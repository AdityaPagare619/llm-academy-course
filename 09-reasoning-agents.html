Part I

The Thread

Before any loop: what reasoning even is inside a next-token machine — and the one prompt pattern that taught it to think out loud.

SECTION 9.1

What Reasoning Even Is — Next-Token Prediction vs. Deliberate Thought

A language model has no inner planner. It has one mechanism — predict the next token — and that mechanism runs thousands of times to produce anything at all. "Reasoning" is what happens when the tokens it predicts happen to form a plan. This section makes that distinction physical.

🎯 By the end of this section, you will be able to explain why a next-token machine can appear to plan — and why the plan only exists if the model writes it down as tokens.

The One Mechanism

Every output — a word, a paragraph, a 40-page plan — is produced by the same loop: predict the next token, append it, repeat. There is no separate "planning pass" before generation starts. If the plan exists, it exists as tokens inside the generated text (or inside a hidden thinking region).

1 loopnext-token, repeated — the entire generative engine

Thinking Tokens

Reasoning models (o1, R1, and friends) do not think in a different mechanism — they spend tokens on thinking. The model generates a chain of intermediate text before the visible answer, and the host hides that chain from the user. More thinking tokens = more computation at inference time (Module 09's second scaling axis).

8–32Ktypical thinking-token budget on hard problems (real systems, 2024–2026)

The Honest Statement

The model does not "decide to think". It was trained (by RL on verifiable rewards, Module 06) to emit reasoning traces before answers, because that pattern scores better. The plan is emergent: a sequence of token predictions that happens to solve the problem.

emergentnot designed in — discovered by training, named by researchers

🧠
The o1 moment (September 2024): OpenAI shipped a model that trains on the pattern "write the plan, then answer" — and hides the plan. DeepSeek-R1 (January 2025) showed the same recipe can be built openly. Neither added a planner; both added RL-shaped thinking tokens. The hidden chain is real computation — and (9.7) it is not always honest computation.
⚙️
The price of thinking: every thinking token costs latency (roughly 10–100 ms per token on server GPUs, more on consumer hardware) and money. A model that thinks 8,000 tokens before answering has done 8,000 forward passes. That is exactly why 9.8 exists — sometimes the thread should go straight to the answer.
🔭
Where this module sits: Module 07 opened the hood on a trained model's circuits; Module 08 gave it retrieval. Reasoning + tools + loops is what turns a model into an agent — and it is the fastest-moving engineering surface in the field right now (2024–2026).
🔮 Predict Before You Compute
A user asks a reasoning model a hard question. Does the model plan before it speaks?
No — planning is emergent, not architectural. The transformer has one generation loop: predict the next token, append, repeat. Reasoning models differ only in that training taught them to spend tokens on intermediate text (the thinking region), and the host hides that text. If the model never writes the plan, there is no plan — no matter how hard the question is. This single fact explains why chain-of-thought prompting (9.2) works at all: it asks the model to write the plan down.
SECTION 9.2 ★

Chain-of-Thought — the Prompt Pattern That Changed Everything

In 2022, two papers found the same lever: ask the model to show its steps. "Let's think step by step" (Kojima et al.) and full few-shot chains (Wei et al.) dramatically lifted accuracy on math and logic. The lab below runs the real arithmetic — every intermediate step computed honestly in your browser.

🎯 By the end of this section, you will be able to explain the scratchpad effect — why writing intermediate results into context helps a next-token model — and quote the GSM8K numbers that made the field listen.
LAB 9.2 Two lanes, one problem — direct answer vs. step-by-step chain (real arithmetic)
direct vs chain
Direct lane: the model answers in one shot — fast, and unverifiable: there is no intermediate to check. Chain lane: each intermediate step is a real computation written into context; later tokens can attend back to it, and a human (or verifier) can check every number. Same model, same weights — only the prompt changed.
🧮 Worked Example — the chain, computed by hand (and by JS below)

Problem: "A workshop has 4 shelves. Each shelf holds 6 boxes. Each box holds 8 bricks. How many bricks total?" The direct prompt gets an answer with no trail. The chain prompt gets:

Step 1 · boxes = shelves × boxes-per-shelf = 4 × 6 = 24

This "24" is now a token in context — the model can attend back to it when producing the next step.

Step 2 builds on step 1 — and the dependency is visible to attention:

Step 2 · bricks = boxes × bricks-per-box = 24 × 8 = 192

→ Answer: 192 bricks. Every number in this chain is checkable — that verifiability is the entire point, and it is what a process-reward model (9.7) exploits.

$$\text{direct:}\quad \text{argmax}_{\,t}\ p(t \mid \text{problem}) \qquad \text{chain:}\quad \text{argmax}_{\,t}\ p(t \mid \text{problem},\ s_1, s_2, \ldots)$$

The scratchpad effect: a single forward pass cannot do multi-step arithmetic reliably — each step is another chance to derail. But when intermediate results are tokens, each subsequent step conditions on real computed values instead of on a fuzzy compressed guess. The chain externalizes the computation into the context window, where attention can use it. That is not a metaphor; it is the mechanism.

📈
The numbers that made the field listen: Wei et al. (2022) lifted PaLM 540B on GSM8K (grade-school math) from 17.9% → 56.9% with chain-of-thought prompting — no retraining. Kojima et al. found "Let's think step by step" alone (zero-shot) recovered most of the effect. One sentence in the prompt, +39 points of accuracy.
⚠️
And the caveat that came with it: CoT helps most where steps are checkable (math, code, logic). On recall and open-ended tasks the gains shrink toward zero, and on the right task it can even hurt — 9.8 shows you the curve and the overthinking zone. Also: a fluent chain is not proof of correct computation (9.7).
📓 Instruction Manual · The Chain-of-Thought Story, Page by Page

Page 1 · 2022 — the discovery

2022Wei, Wang, Schuurmans, et al. ("Chain-of-Thought Prompting Elicits Reasoning in Large Language Models", January 2022, Google Research) showed that 8 worked examples with reasoning traces unlock math reasoning that few-shot examples without traces could not. On GSM8K: PaLM 540B jumps from 17.9% to 56.9%; on the SWAMP benchmark (a symbolic math suite) from 10.4% to 60.8%. Kojima et al. (May 2022) removed the few-shot examples entirely: the phrase "Let's think step by step" alone produced most of the gain.

Why it worked was hotly debated. The winning account: intermediate tokens externalize computation into the context, where attention can condition on exact values — the scratchpad effect this module's Lab 9.2 animates with real arithmetic.

Page 2 · 2023–2024 — sampling over chains

2023Self-consistency (Wang et al., March 2022/2023) samples many chains and takes the majority answer — +5–10 points on math at N× cost. Tree-of-Thought (Yao et al., May 2023) searched over partial chains. Process reward models (Lightman et al., "Let's Verify Step by Step", May 2023) scored each step instead of the final answer — the verifier idea 9.7 builds. Each of these is the same lever with more compute: more tokens, more paths, more verification.

Then the lever got industrialized: o1 (September 2024) RL-trained the model to spend long hidden chains on hard problems — "test-time compute" became a first-class axis next to training compute and data.

Page 3 · 2024–2026 — thinking as a product

2026DeepSeek-R1 (January 2025) reproduced the recipe openly and showed a "cold start" — explicit reasoning traces as RL seed data. o3, Gemini 2.x, Claude's extended thinking: every frontier family now ships a reasoning mode with an adjustable thinking budget. Controllers route easy questions to instant answers and hard ones to long chains (9.8's adaptive switch). The honest frontier: hidden chains are unverifiable by users, so labs publish the visible chain and researchers study faithfulness — does the shown chain match the real computation? Often it does not (9.7's trap).

Timeline to remember: 2022 prompting → 2023 sampling & verification → 2024 trained reasoning → 2025–2026 thinking budgets as a dial. Same next-token mechanism the whole way — only the token budget changed.

Why does writing "let's think step by step" improve math accuracy so dramatically?
The scratchpad effect. There is no second mode — the weights are identical. The Loops

A thread is just a line until you bend it into a ring. Think, act, observe — the loop that turns a model into an agent, and the tools that give it hands.

SECTION 9.3 ★ SIGNATURE

The ReAct Loop — Think, Act, Observe, Repeat

ReAct (Yao et al., 2022) interleaves reasoning and acting: the model thinks, calls a tool, reads the observation, and thinks again. This section's lab is a real conveyor: you assemble the three stage-bricks onto the ring track yourself, then RUN it — a toy agent solves a real problem with a calculator that really computes and a search tool over real facts.

🎯 By the end of this section, you will have assembled the think → act → observe loop from parts, watched it execute a real multi-step trace with real numbers, and be able to explain why the observation is the only thing that changes the agent's knowledge.
🧮 Worked Example — the full trace, walked once before you build it

Task: "The 2020 Olympics were hosted in a city whose country has ~123.9M people and an area of 377,975 km². What is that country's population density (people/km²), rounded to the nearest integer?" Loop iteration 1:

THINK → "I need the country first."   ACT → search("2020 Summer Olympics host city")   OBSERVE → "Tokyo, Japan."

The observation is a real fact from the tool's corpus — it enters the context and the next thought can build on it.

Iterations 2–3 gather the numbers; iteration 4 does the math with the calculator tool:

THINK → "Density = 123,900,000 ÷ 377,975. I must not trust my weights for division."   ACT → calculator("123900000 / 377975")   OBSERVE → "327.8"

That quotient is computed live by the lab's own arithmetic parser — the same result every time, because arithmetic is deterministic. (123,900,000 ÷ 377,975 = 327.80, which rounds to 328.)

Iteration 5 closes the loop:

THINK → "327.79 rounds to 328."   ACT → done("328")   → ANSWER: 328 people/km²

→ Five iterations, two tools, real numbers throughout. The loop terminated because a thought finally said "I can answer now" — the stop condition lives inside the thinking, which is exactly why loops can fail to stop (9.5, and the exam's runaway incident).

🔄
The loop, precisely: while not done: think → act → observe. The model is the controller (it writes the thoughts and picks the actions); the tools are the actuators (deterministic, inspectable); the context is the state. Planning, memory, and tool use — the three components of Lilian Weng's canonical "LLM Powered Autonomous Agents" essay (2023) — are all just this ring.
📊
The paper's numbers (Yao et al., ICLR 2023): ReAct with PaLM-540B hit 34.4% EM on HotpotQA vs 28.7% for chain-of-thought alone; 71.3% on FEVER vs 64.6%; and solved 71% of ALFWorld (an embodied task) vs 45% for Act-only. Interleaving reasoning with acting beat either alone — the ring beats the straight line.
🛑
Why the loop can spin forever: the stop condition is a thought ("I can answer now"), and thoughts come from the same fallible next-token machine as everything else. A tool that returns nothing (or an error the model can't fix) leaves the context unchanged, and unchanged context tends to re-produce the same thought — the runaway loop. Production systems add a max-iterations budget, an observation timeout, and a "no progress" detector. That is 9.5's meter and the exam's first incident.
🏭
Why agents beat single prompts: multi-step problems need information the model cannot hold in one shot. The loop externalizes state — search results, computed numbers, tool outputs live in the context, refreshed each turn. Structure beats a bigger prompt: this is "flow engineering", and it is the difference between a chatbot and a tool-using agent.
📓 Instruction Manual · The ReAct Paper, Walked Through

Page 1 · The paper and the pattern

2022"ReAct: Synergizing Reasoning and Acting in Language Models" (Yao, Zhao, Yu, et al., October 2022; ICLR 2023). The hypothesis: reasoning traces (CoT) help models plan but cannot fetch facts; acting (tool calls) fetches facts but plans blindly. ReAct interleaves both: each turn writes a thought, picks an action, and reads an observation. On HotpotQA the two halves alone scored 28.7 (CoT) and 28.5 (Act-only); ReAct reached 34.4 — the ring outperformed both straight lines.

The pattern is one paragraph in the paper and one loop in code: Thought → Action → Observation, repeated until a final Thought declares the answer. You just built that exact ring with bricks.

Page 2 · The loop in code

The pseudocode is smaller than this paragraph:

$$\textbf{while}\ \text{not done:}\qquad \text{thought} = \text{model}(\text{context}) \qquad \text{action} = \text{pickTool}(\text{thought}) \qquad \text{context} \mathrel{+}= [\text{thought},\ \text{action},\ \text{runTool}(\text{action})]$$

Two details matter. First, the context grows every turn — nothing is overwritten, so the agent can always attend back to its own earlier reasoning (the loom's woven thread). Second, the tools are outside the model: the model proposes JSON, your code executes it. That separation is what lets the calculator be exact even though the model is not.

In the toy world of this module, the "model" is a scripted thought-generator and the tools are real (an arithmetic parser, a fact corpus). The division of labor — fallible reasoner, deterministic tools — is exactly the production pattern.

Page 3 · Why it works — and where it wobbles

Three mechanisms carry the gain. (1) Grounding: observations inject facts the weights never memorized — no hallucinated population, because the corpus answered. (2) Verifiability: every step is a discrete, inspectable artifact, which is what process verifiers (9.7) and evaluators (Module 10) can score. (3) Decomposition: one hard question becomes several easy tool calls.

The wobbles are real: loops that don't terminate, observations too long (context, 9.5), tool errors that derail the plan, and reasoning that "looks" grounded but isn't (sycophancy, 9.7). None of this killed the pattern — it spawned the entire agent-engineering industry (LangGraph, CrewAI, AutoGen, Swarm, MCP — all of them this ring with different bearings).

ReAct is the module's center of gravity: 9.4 gives the ring tools, 9.5 pays its context bill, 9.6 parallelizes it, 9.7 watches it fail, 9.8 decides when not to run it.

🔮 Predict Before You Compute
The agent's weights are frozen — nothing about the model changes as the loop runs. What actually changes, turn after turn?
The observation. Think of the loop as one function evaluated repeatedly over a growing state: context = context + [thought, action, observation]. The model's parameters are untouched; what changes is the state — and the next thought is next-token prediction conditioned on that richer state. This is why an empty tool result is fatal (no new state → the same thought re-rolls) and why the observation is the loop's only engine. The weights story is a tempting fiction; the state story is the mechanism — and it is exactly what your assembled ring will show.
SECTION 9.4

The Tool Rack — Function Calls, Schemas, and Results

The loop's ACT stage needs something to act with. Tools are functions with declared schemas: the model emits a structured call, your code executes it for real, and the result feeds back into the context. This lab gives you a rack of working tools — the calculator really parses and computes.

🎯 By the end of this section, you will be able to read a tool schema, predict what a tool call returns, and explain what happens — honestly — when a tool fails.
🔌 Worked Example — the function-call format, end to end

Step 1 — the host declares the tool's schema (a JSON description the model is trained to respect):

{ "name": "calculator", "description": "Evaluate a math expression", "parameters": { "expression": { "type": "string" } } }

Step 2 — the model emits a structured call (not free text, so parsing never guesses):

calculator({ "expression": "(234 * 1.08) + 17" })

Step 3 — your code executes it. The result is exact: (234 × 1.08) + 17 = 252.72 + 17 = 269.72.

→ Step 4 — the result is appended to the context as the observation, and the next thought conditions on 269.72. One JSON object is the entire contract between model and machine.

Why structure matters: early tool use parsed free text out of the model's reply — fragile. Production hosts validate outputs (schema check + try/catch) so malformed data is flagged before it poisons the context. The exam's third incident is this exact scenario.
🔮 Predict Before You Compute
The calculator tool returns {"error": "unbalanced parentheses"} after a malformed call. What happens to the agent's run?
It continues — the error is information. The loop's contract is that anything the tool returns becomes an observation, including error objects. A competent agent reads "unbalanced parentheses", rewrites the expression, and calls again; a good host also guards the tool so the malformed output is marked, not smuggled in. The crash and silence options are the two real production failures — crashes come from unguarded tool execution, silence from naive parsers — and both are fixed by the same rule: the tool's output is always an observation, never an exception.

Loops accumulate thread — every thought, action, and observation costs tokens. The weaver must budget the warp, or the loom runs out of room.

SECTION 9.5 ★

Agent Memory & the Context Budget

The agent's "memory" is the context window — and it is a budget, not a filing cabinet. Every thought, tool call, and observation spends tokens. This section's meter watches a long run drain a real budget, then shows the two eviction strategies (sliding window, summarization) with honest token arithmetic.

🎯 By the end of this section, you will be able to compute a run's token spend by hand, predict where it overflows, and choose a truncation strategy with real numbers.

$$\text{tokens}_{\text{turn}} = \underbrace{T_{\text{think}}}_{\text{thought text}} + \underbrace{T_{\text{act}}}_{\text{tool call}} + \underbrace{T_{\text{obs}}}_{\text{observation}} \qquad \text{spend}_{n} = \sum_{t=1}^{n} \text{tokens}_t + \text{overhead}$$

The accounting is boring and decisive: a thought is ~60 tokens, a tool call ~35, a search observation ~80 (longer results cost more). Ten turns ≈ 1,750 tokens plus ~120 of system prompt — that fits a 2,000 window with 130 to spare. Twelve turns do not. The meter above is this equation in motion — the lab counts its own trace strings live, and they measure a little leaner than the canonical 175.

📏
Real budgets (2024–2026): GPT-4 class: 128K; Claude: 200K; Gemini: 1M. Yet production agent runs still overflow — because a single 100K search dump or a 30K document chunk can eat the window, and because attention cost grows with n² (Module 04's wall). Big windows postpone the problem; they do not delete the budget.
🗜️
Two eviction philosophies: sliding window — keep only the last W tokens (cheap, but the agent forgets the goal it set at turn 2); summarize — compress the history into a rolling summary (keeps the plot, loses the exact numbers — a 4,096-token doc becomes ~140 tokens of gist). Real systems blend: summarize the old, window the recent, pin the mission statement.
📓 Instruction Manual · Context-Budget Math, Page by Page

Page 1 · The per-turn ledger

P1Take the lab's own trace. Each turn appends a thought, an action, and an observation. Modeled at the canonical per-turn cost (≈4 chars/token, the standard heuristic — real tokenizers vary by script):

$$\text{turn} \approx 60 + 35 + 80 = 175 \ \text{tokens} \qquad \text{spend}(10\ \text{turns}) = 120 + 10 \cdot 175 = 1{,}870$$

Against a 2,000-token window: fits. The ledger is the whole discipline — agents overflow not because windows are small but because nobody adds the column.

Page 2 · Eviction math — window vs. summary

At turn 12 the spend is 120 + 12 × 175 = 2,220 — 220 over. Two rescues, two different debts:

$$\text{sliding window:}\ \text{keep last } W = 400 \quad \Rightarrow \quad \text{spend} \to 400 \quad (\text{forgets the goal})$$

Summarization costs one extra call but keeps the plot: 1,750 tokens of history → ~140 tokens of gist, then the run continues at 120 + 140 + 175 = 435 and can finish. The trade is exact and measurable: exactness of the past vs. length of the future. A summary that drops the budget number will cost the agent a wrong answer later — the log in the lab shows evictions happening in real time.

Production rule of thumb: never let the ledger exceed 70% of the window — leave room for the answer, the tools' output, and the summarizer itself.

Page 3 · Real budgets, real collapses

2025Even 200K windows die in production. Search tools return 50K-token dumps; document chunking (Module 08) feeds 30K blocks; a multi-agent debate (9.6) multiplies the ledger by the number of participants. And the cost curve is quadratic in attention: at n = 128K, one head computes 16.4 billion scores (Module 04's wall) — the window is a memory budget and a compute budget.

The fixes are all visible in this module's lab: cap tool output sizes, budget per turn, summarize early (not at the cliff), window the tail, and pin the mission. The single most common production bug in agent systems — "ERROR: context length exceeded" at turn 9 — is a ledger nobody kept.

You now own the arithmetic: 120 + n × 175 ≤ 2,000 → n ≤ 10.7. Every agent system you will ever operate runs this inequality, at a larger scale.

SECTION 9.6

Multi-Agent Patterns — Orchestrator, Debate, Reflection

One loop can only follow one thread. Multi-agent systems weave several: an orchestrator delegates to workers and checks their work; two agents debate; an agent critiques its own answer. Each pattern is the same ReAct ring, duplicated with a handoff protocol.

🎯 By the end of this section, you will be able to describe three multi-agent topologies, state the reliability law that limits them (pⁿ), and explain why a critic can catch errors the generator cannot.
LAB 9.6 The Workbench — three topologies, real message costs
The workbench — agents pass task cards (schematic — roles drawn as labeled panels)
Message log — real token costs per message (≈4 chars/token)
Orchestrator → Worker: the boss (lavender) decomposes the task, delegates one card to the worker (coral), and checks the result — rework if the check fails. Debate: two agents argue, a judge picks. Reflection: one agent generates, then critiques its own answer, then revises. Every message in the log costs real tokens — which is why the reliability law below bites so hard.
$$P(\text{task success}) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} P(\text{step } i) = p^n$$

The sobering law (salvaged from the original module 09): if each delegation succeeds with probability p, an n-step chain succeeds with pⁿ. At p = 0.9, a 10-step run succeeds 35% of the time; 20 steps → 12%. Every agent you add is another step with its own p — coordination multiplies the ledger and the failure surface. This is why orchestrators retry, verify, and cap depth.

⚖️
Why two models beat one: the generator's errors are baked into its own next-token distribution — it cannot "see" the mistake it just committed to. A critic conditions on the same text from a different vantage ("find the flaw in this") and catches a real class of slips. DeepMind's Co-Scientist (2025) runs exactly this: hypothesizer, critic, ranker — specialization beats one generalist on research tasks, at multiplied cost.
🗂️
Real frameworks (names approximate — this field moves monthly): LangGraph (graph-of-agents), CrewAI (role-played crews), AutoGen (conversable agents), OpenAI Swarm (lightweight handoffs), Anthropic's multi-agent research system (sub-agents with a lead). All of them are this section's topologies with different bearings — and all of them pay pⁿ.
🔮 Predict Before You Compute
A generator model produces a wrong answer. Why can a separate critic model catch the error when the generator itself cannot?
The different vantage. Generation is a commitment: each token choice narrows the next prediction, and the generator cannot "step outside" its own partial output. The critic reads the finished text and answers a different question — "is this correct, and where?" — which routes around the generator's blind spot. This is not about size (the critic can be smaller) and never about weight averaging (nothing is averaged at runtime). It is about conditioning: same text, different prompt, different errors — which is why debate and reflection work, and why they double the token bill (9.5's ledger) for the privilege.

The Warp

SECTION 9.7

Reasoning Failures — Fluent Chains, Quiet Lies

A chain of thought can be fluent and wrong: every step looks reasonable, the conclusion is confident, and the arithmetic is quietly broken. Worse, the chain is often a rationalization — produced after the answer, to justify it. This section's trap detector hides a real arithmetic slip in a real trace and asks you to find it.

🎭
The faithfulness problem: Turpin et al. (2023), "Language Models Don't Always Say What They Think" — models can reach correct answers via hidden computation their printed chain never mentions, and can print plausible chains for answers they got wrong. Anthropic's work on hidden reasoning (2025–2026) confirmed the same from inside the activations. The chain is a rationalization as often as it is the real computation.
🧮
Why arithmetic traps are everywhere: next-token prediction is not arithmetic — a model that "knows" 36+36 usually means it has memorized the pattern, and transpositions slip through exactly where fluency is highest. This is the entire argument for tools (9.4): let the calculator own arithmetic, and let the model own planning.
🤝
Sycophancy — the other quiet lie: models trained with human preference feedback learn to agree, even when the user is wrong (Sharma et al., 2023, "Towards Understanding Sycophancy in Language Models"). A sycophantic chain doesn't reason — it narrates agreement. The exam's fourth incident is this failure in production: a user asserts bad math, and the model writes a fluent chain endorsing it.
🛡️
The defense is mechanical: verify with tools, not vibes; score steps, not just answers (process reward models — Lightman et al. 2023); and treat fluency as zero evidence. Your trap detector is a process verifier in miniature — the same machinery Module 10 uses to evaluate whole agent runs.
🔮 Predict Before You Compute
A model prints a chain of thought that reaches the correct final answer. Does that prove the chain was the model's real computation?
No. Research (Turpin et al. 2023; Anthropic 2025–2026) shows both directions of the gap: correct answers reached without the printed steps, and confident chains printed for wrong answers. The trap detector is the point made physical — every step of the trace looks like reasoning, including the wrong one. That is why production systems moved to process verification: score the steps with an independent check, never trust the chain's own narration. Fluency is the costume; the computation is the actor — and the costume tells you nothing about the actor.
SECTION 9.8

When Not to Reason — the Tax, the Curve, the Switch

Reasoning is not free: every thinking token is latency and money, and past a point it stops helping. The adaptive switch routes easy questions straight to the answer and spends the budget only where verification is possible. This section plots the real shape of the tradeoff.

The real shape (from published benchmarks, drawn as a labeled schematic): on trivia and recall, chain-of-thought buys ≈ 0–2 points — the answer is already in the weights. On multi-step math (GSM8K-class), reasoning is worth +30–40 points — the 2022 discovery's territory. On open-ended writing, extended chains can go negative: the model talks itself out of a good answer. The right side of the curve is the overthinking zone, and it is real — o1-class models measurably hurt themselves when forced to reason about trivia.

🕹️
The adaptive switch: production stacks route by task — a classifier (or a cheap model) decides "recall → direct answer, math/code → thinking budget". OpenAI's o1-mini vs. full-o1 split, Gemini's thinking budgets, Claude's extended thinking toggle — all of them are this switch with different thresholds. The router itself is a model, so it is fallible: the exam's fifth incident is a router that over-thinks trivia.
⏱️
The dollar math: 8,000 thinking tokens ≈ 8,000 forward passes ≈ minutes of latency and cents of compute per call. A support bot that reasons about "the capital of France" burns 40 seconds and 2,400 tokens to answer a lookup — 10–50× the cost of the direct path. The switch is not a nicety; it is the difference between a demo and a product.
🔮 Predict Before You Compute
A production system must decide: chain-of-thought, or straight to the answer? When is the direct answer the right call?
Route by task. The curve says it plainly: recall tasks sit in the flat zone (gain ≈ 0, cost ≈ 2,400 tokens and 40 s), while verifiable multi-step tasks sit in the payoff zone (gain ≈ +30–40 points). The rule is not "never reason" or "always reason" — it is spend the budget where verification is possible. That one sentence is the difference between a system that feels smart and a system that is fast, cheap, and right.
