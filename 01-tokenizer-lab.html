SECTION 1.1

The Naive Approach: One Number Per Word

The simplest idea: assign every unique word an ID, then a corner of space. This fails catastrophically — and seeing why geometrically is the key to everything that follows.

Three Fatal Problems

1. Vocabulary explosion. English has ~500K words, plus names, typos, and COVID-19-era neologisms. Your vocabulary grows without bound.

2. No similarity. In one-hot encoding, every word gets its own perpendicular axis. cat is exactly as far from dog as it is from refrigerator. The geometry encodes zero meaning.

3. Out-of-vocabulary (OOV). A word the model has never seen gets... nothing. No axis exists for it. No representation at all.

💡
The fix (spoiler): Split words into subword pieces. "unbelievable" → ["un", "believ", "able"]. Now rare words share pieces with common ones, and the vocabulary stays small. That's what BPE does — Section 1.3.

SECTION 1.2

The Statistical Era: N-Gram Language Models

Before neural networks, we predicted text by counting. A bigram model asks: given the previous character, what's the most likely next one? This is the ancestor of every LLM — and you can train one in 20 lines of code.

$$P(x_t \mid x_{t-1}) = \frac{\text{count}(x_{t-1}, x_t)}{\text{count}(x_{t-1})}$$

Perplexity measures how "surprised" the model is: PPL = e^{-\frac{1}{N}\sum \log P(x_t \mid x_{t-1})}. Lower = better. A perplexity of 10 means the model is as confused as if choosing uniformly among 10 options each step.

🔗
Why this matters: An LLM is exactly this — a next-token predictor — except the "counts" are computed by a neural network over a context of thousands of tokens instead of a table over 1 token of history. Same objective: minimize −log P(correct\ next).

The sparsity curse

A trigram model over a 50K-word vocabulary has 50K³ = 125 trillion possible rows. Almost all are zero — no amount of data fills them. This is why n-grams cap out around n=3, and why we needed neural networks.

SECTION 1.3 ★ SIGNATURE LAB

Byte-Pair Encoding: The Algorithm That Powers GPT

BPE starts with individual character-bricks and repeatedly fuses the most frequent adjacent pair into one wider brick. Common words become single tokens; rare words stay as loose pieces. This is the exact algorithm GPT-2, GPT-4, and LLaMA use — watch it weld, merge by merge.

Click Shatter into character-bricks to split the corpus above into its raw characters — the starting vocabulary of any BPE tokenizer.

The Algorithm

1. Split corpus into characters
2. Count every adjacent pair
3. Merge the most frequent pair → new token
4. Repeat until vocab target reached

$$\text{compression ratio} = \frac{\text{original chars}}{\text{tokens after BPE}}$$

Real systems: GPT-2's vocabulary is 50,257 tokens — 256 bytes + <|endoftext|> + 50,000 learned merges, trained on 40GB of text. LLaMA-3 uses 128K tokens. The merge list is the tokenizer — loading it means replaying every weld in order.

🧠
Deep insight: BPE is a compression algorithm — invented in 1994, for data compression, not language. The model sees a compressed view of text. This is why tokenization quirks — like " strawberry" splitting into ["st","raw","berry"] — cause the model to lose track of individual letters.

BPE isn't the only way to pick merges. BERT uses WordPiece — same fuse-the-bricks idea, but it scores pairs by how much they'd improve the model's likelihood of the data, not by raw frequency. Watch both algorithms race on the identical corpus and pick different first moves.

BPE always fuses the pair that appears most often. WordPiece fuses the pair whose merge gives the biggest jump in likelihood score — score(a,b) = count(a,b) / (count(a) · count(b)) — which favors pairs that are rare individually but strongly stick together, like "q" + "u".

SECTION 1.5

Tokenization in the Wild: Glitches, Taxes & Templates

Where tokenization stops being theory and starts breaking production systems.

😵 Glitch Tokens

SolidGoldMagikarp and petertodd are real tokens in GPT's vocabulary that cause bizarre behavior:

SolidGoldMagikarp petertodd

They appear in the vocabulary (scraped from Reddit usernames) but were almost never trained on — so their embeddings sit near-random in space. Ask GPT-3 to repeat one and it hallucinates. Lesson: a token with no training signal is undefined behavior, not zero behavior.

📨 Chat Templates

Chat models don't see "a conversation" — they see one long string with special tokens marking turns:

<|im_start|>user
Hello!<|im_end|>
<|im_start|>assistant
Hi!<|im_end|>

ChatML (OpenAI), [INST] (LLaMA-2), <start_of_turn> (Gemini) — different formats, same idea. Get this wrong and the model treats your chat as garbled pretraining text.

🍓 The Strawberry Problem

Ask GPT "how many r's in strawberry?" and it often says 2. It never sees letters — only tokens:

The letter "r" is buried inside different tokens at different positions. Counting requires memorizing each token's spelling — which the model mostly hasn't done.

Why our MicroGPT uses character-level: With only 6.3M parameters trained for minutes, a subword model can't learn 32K embeddings AND language at the same time. Character-level (80 tokens) lets it focus all capacity on patterns. The cost: it reads "hello" as 5 tokens instead of 1 — so attention must learn spelling and meaning.
⚠️
Why byte-level produces garbage: Bytes 128–255 only form valid text in multi-byte UTF-8 sequences. A byte-level model must learn English and UTF-8 grammar simultaneously. Sample a lead byte with no valid continuation and you get mojibake. Character-level sidesteps this completely.
