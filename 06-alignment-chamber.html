Part I

The Problem

A next-token predictor is not a helper. The objective-function gap — and why the field's canonical cautionary tale is a paperclip factory.

SECTION 6.1

Why Align at All — The Objective-Function Problem

Pretraining optimizes one number: next-token cross-entropy over internet text. That objective learns to predict text — flattery, deception, and harm included. It never learns to care about you. This section states the gap precisely, meets the canonical cautionary tale, and sets the target: HHH.

🎯 By the end of this section, you will be able to state why next-token loss is not a helpfulness objective, explain the paperclip loop as a labeled thought experiment, and name the HHH taxonomy.

The Objective-Function Gap

A model trained to predict text learns the statistics of text. "Help me with X" is followed in the corpus by more requests and generic continuations far more often than by actual assistance. The loss rewards text-likeness — not truth, kindness, or safety. Alignment is the art of adding objectives the pretraining loss never contained.

1 objectivenext-token loss — and it does not know what you meant

The Paperclip Maximizer THOUGHT EXPERIMENT

A hypothetical AI given one goal — maximize paperclips — and no guardrails. It converts every available resource into clips, including the atoms of its own builders. The lesson is not that AI wants paperclips; it is that an objective, optimized hard enough, will be gamed — and the proxy's success can be the real goal's destruction.

1 goaloptimized without a definition of "enough"

HHH — The Working Target

The field's shorthand for what alignment wants: Helpful (does what you asked, usefully), Honest (doesn't invent or mislead), Harmless (doesn't hurt you or others). Every method in this module — SFT, reward models, RLHF, DPO, Constitutional AI — is a mechanism for pushing a model toward HHH.

A model is trained on the entire internet to minimize next-token cross-entropy. When you ask it "Please help me with my homework", what is the model actually optimizing for when it responds?
The likely continuation of text. Cross-entropy rewards matching the training distribution — nothing else. The model has never seen a reward for "helpful", only for "predictable". If the most likely continuation after your question is another question, it produces another question. This gap between "predicts text" and "does what you want" is the entire reason this module exists.

Why the raw model does this

The internet is mostly text about things, not answers to questions. A raw model has seen "Please write a poem" thousands of times — almost always followed by another line of text, not a poem. SECTION 6.3 ★

SFT — Supervised Fine-Tuning

The first stage: cross-entropy on curated (prompt, ideal-response) pairs. It is exactly the Module 05 next-token loss — the only thing that changes is the data. This lab computes the real loss on a toy response, then trains one gradient step so you can watch the numbers move.

🎯 By the end of this section, you will be able to compute the SFT loss for any token by hand, explain what SFT can and cannot teach, and name InstructGPT's first stage.
🧮 Worked Example — SFT cross-entropy by hand: the demo response vs a rejected continuation

Prompt: "Q: Capital of France? A:" — the model's logits for the first answer token, over our six-token toy vocabulary:

logits = [Paris 3.0, France 1.0, Berlin 0.5, Rome −1.0, Madrid −2.0, <end> −3.0]

Softmax: e³ = 20.09, e¹ = 2.72, e^0.5 = 1.65, e⁻¹ = 0.37, e⁻² = 0.14, e⁻³ = 0.05 → sum = 25.01. So p(Paris) = 0.803, p(France) = 0.109.

Demo loss (target = "Paris"): −ln(0.803) = 0.219

Rejected loss (target = "France"): −ln(0.109) = 2.219

The gradient is the difference between prediction and target — for cross-entropy with a softmax head, ∂L/∂z = p − y:

∂L/∂z_Paris = 0.803 − 1 = −0.197  ·  ∂L/∂z_France = 0.109 − 0 = +0.109

Paris's logit rises (loss shrinks), France's logit falls (loss would grow) — one gradient step on the demo response simultaneously makes the rejected continuation less likely.

→ Total demo response: 0.219 (Paris) + 0.219 (<end>) ≈ 0.44. Total rejected: 2.219 + 0.457 ≈ 2.68. Six times the loss. That gap is the entire mechanism of SFT: better data, same loss, huge behavioral difference.

Average negative log-likelihood of the target tokens. The only change from pretraining: (x, y) are now curated (instruction, ideal-response) pairs written or heavily edited by humans — the Lab 6.3 demo path instead of the internet.

Page 2 · What the Data Change Does

Pretraining data answers the question "what text follows this text?" SFT data answers "what should a helpful assistant say here?" The model cannot tell the losses apart — it just sees a different distribution and shifts its probabilities. This is why Lab 6.2's raw model continues while the SFT model answers: the conditional P(response | question) got reweighted.

InstructGPT stage 1: ~13k demonstrations (approx), written by 40 contractors. That tiny dataset produced the entire "answer the question" format shift. InstructGPT's paper reports SFT alone already made a dramatic difference on the API prompt distribution.

Page 3 · Quality Beats Quantity — And the Ceiling

LIMA (2023) built a strong assistant from just 1,000 carefully curated examples — a result that shaped Alpaca (52k, self-instructed), Vicuna, and the open-assistant wave. But SFT has a hard ceiling: the loss only rewards imitating good responses. Nothing in it says "this response is better than that one".

SFT can copy excellence; it cannot rank it. Preference — "A is better than B" — never appears in a cross-entropy loss. That is exactly the gap 6.4 and 6.5 exist to close.

SECTION 6.4 ★

The Reward Model — Bradley-Terry Pairwise Preference

SFT can imitate; it cannot prefer. The reward model (RM) learns preference: given two responses to the same prompt, it learns to score the winner higher. The math is 70 years old — the Bradley-Terry model — and it is still the heart of RLHF. This lab trains a toy RM on your clicks, with the real loss.

🎯 By the end of this section, you will be able to compute a Bradley-Terry probability by hand, derive its training loss, and state precisely what an RM does — and does not — learn to predict.
🧮 Worked Example — Bradley-Terry by hand: one pair, one probability, one loss

The RM gives each response a scalar score: r_A = 2.5, r_B = 1.0. Bradley-Terry turns the score difference into a preference probability via the sigmoid:

P(A ≻ B) = σ(r_A − r_B) = σ(1.5) = 1 / (1 + e^{−1.5}) = 0.818

A margin of 1.5 means "A beats B" about 82% of the time. The margin, not the raw scores, is what matters — scores can shift by a constant with zero effect on preferences.

Training: one human click "A is better" becomes one label, and the RM minimizes negative log-likelihood:

L = −log σ(r_A − r_B) = −ln(0.818) = 0.201

The gradient pushes r_A up and r_B down — proportionally to how surprised the model was:

∂L/∂r_A = σ(r_A − r_B) − 1 = −0.182  ·  ∂L/∂r_B = 1 − σ(r_A − r_B) = +0.182

→ If the RM had predicted 50/50, the update would be 0.5 — maximal learning. If it already predicted 95%, the update is 0.05 — it is already sure. The sigmoid makes the RM learn fastest where it is most wrong.

📓 Instruction Manual · Bradley-Terry, Derived

Page 1 · Preference as a Ratio of Strengths

B1Bradley and Terry (1952) modeled pairwise comparisons in sports: team i beats team j with probability proportional to its strength s_i:

$$P(i \succ j) = \frac{s_i}{s_i + s_j}$$

Substitute log-strengths s_i = e^{r_i}:

$$P(i \succ j) = \frac{e^{r_i}}{e^{r_i} + e^{r_j}} = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(r_i - r_j)}} = \sigma(r_i - r_j)$$

The sigmoid of the score margin. In RLHF, r is the reward model's scalar; the human "match" is the comparison. This is not an analogy — InstructGPT's RM used exactly this objective.

Page 2 · The Training Loss and Its Gradient

One labeled pair (x, y_w, y_l) — winner and loser — gives one loss term:

$$\mathcal{L}_{RM} = -\log \sigma\big(r_\phi(x, y_w) - r_\phi(x, y_l)\big)$$

The gradient (derived in the worked example): ∂L/∂r_w = σ(r_w − r_l) − 1 and ∂L/∂r_l = 1 − σ(r_w − r_l). Winner's score rises, loser's falls, and the step size is largest when the model was most wrong. After training, r(x, y) is a scalar "goodness" proxy for any candidate response — the reward that 6.5 will optimize.

Page 3 · What the RM Actually Predicts

Important precision: the RM is trained on relative labels, so its absolute scale is arbitrary — only margins matter. It predicts the odds that one response is preferred over another, not an absolute quality meter, and it inherits whatever the labelers agreed on:

Real preference data is noisy — labeler agreement on which response is better is often only ~60–80% (approx). The RM learns the average preference, which is why it can be fooled by length and formatting: if most labelers mildly prefer longer answers, the RM encodes "longer = better" as a spurious cue. You will weaponize exactly this in the exam.
Page 1 of 3
$$P(y_w \succ y_l \mid x) = \sigma\big(r_\phi(x, y_w) - r_\phi(x, y_l)\big)$$

Why a scalar, and not a classifier that says "good/bad"? The RLHF loop needs a number it can differentiate and optimize. A binary classifier gives you a category; a Bradley-Terry score gives you a smooth gradient field over response space — the "north" that 6.5's policy climbs.

🗂️
Where the pairs come from in the real world: Anthropic's HH-RLHF dataset — 161k human preference pairs (approx) — trained generations of reward models. InstructGPT used tens of thousands of comparisons written by its labelers (approx). Preference data is the most expensive ingredient in the entire pipeline — remember that when 6.9 asks which method needs the most.
🔮 Predict Before You Compute
A reward model finishes training on pairwise comparisons. What exactly has it learned to predict?
Relative preference odds. Bradley-Terry training only ever sees comparisons, so the RM's scale is arbitrary — add a constant to all scores and every probability is unchanged. It models "A is preferred to B" with σ(r_A − r_B), full stop. This is why the RM can be gamed (length, formatting, sycophancy): those are real statistical patterns in the labelers' preferences, and the RM faithfully encodes them. 6.5's KL anchor exists because of this.

RLHF — The PPO Loop

The loop that created ChatGPT: the policy generates a response, the reward model scores it, the advantage tells the policy how much better than average it did, and a clipped update nudges the policy — while a KL anchor keeps it from drifting into reward hacking. PPO's L^CLIP — drag the probability ratio ρ on the canvas
1.0
inside the fence — full gradient · drag ρ past 1.2 and the objective flattens
$$L^{CLIP} = \mathbb{E}\Big[\min\big(\rho_t \hat A_t,\; \mathrm{clip}(\rho_t, 1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon)\, \hat A_t\big)\Big]$$

Read it slowly: ρ_t = π_θ(y|x) / π_old(y|x) is how much the new policy changed its odds on this response. If the change is inside [0.8, 1.2], the objective is the plain ρ·A — full credit. Outside, the clip caps the credit: the update can keep improving the response, but it can't keep inflating its probability this step. One number — ε ≈ 0.2 — keeps PPO from throwing the policy across the room.

Drag the point: with A > 0 the policy wants ρ big — but beyond 1.2 the objective flattens. With A < 0 it wants ρ small — but not below 0.8. The flat shoulders are the safety: even a wildly wrong update can't move the policy more than the clip allows.
📓 Instruction Manual · The RLHF Objective, Term by Term

Page 1 · Maximize Reward, But Stay Near the Reference

R1The full RLHF objective, as it appears in InstructGPT (and in every RLHF paper since):

$$\max_{\theta}\; \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D},\, y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot|x)}\Big[\, r_\phi(x, y)\,\Big] - \beta\; D_{KL}\big(\pi_\theta(\cdot|x) \,\|\, \pi_{ref}(\cdot|x)\big)$$

Two terms with two jobs. The first: generate responses the reward model scores highly — that is the alignment signal, the "north" from 6.4. The second: don't drift too far from π_ref, the frozen SFT model we already verified. β weighs the leash.

Page 2 · Why the KL Anchor Is Non-Negotiable

The reward model is a proxy. Optimize any proxy hard enough and you find the exploit — text the RM loves and humans hate: verbosity (labelers mildly prefer longer answers, so the RM encodes it), sycophancy (agreement scores higher than honesty), formatting tricks, even gibberish with the right surface cues.

$$D_{KL}\big(\pi_\theta \,\|\, \pi_{ref}\big) = \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta}\Big[\log \frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{ref}(y|x)}\Big]$$

The KL term charges rent for every probability the policy moves. Small moves toward reward are cheap; big moves into RM-blind-spot territory are expensive. Without it (β = 0), the loop optimizes the proxy to the point of breaking — you will watch exactly this in Lab 6.5 and diagnose it in the exam.

Page 3 · The Real Pipelines That Run This

InstructGPT (2022): SFT → RM → PPO with ε = 0.2 and β ≈ 0.02 (approx), trained on the API prompt distribution. Llama-2-Chat (2023): SFT → rejection sampling → RLHF with two reward models — one for helpfulness, one for safety — blended, then PPO against them, with the safety RM also used to re-rank samples.

The PPO-specific machinery — the ratio ρ, the clip, the advantage estimator — exists because RL is sample-hungry and unstable. The clip (Lab 6.5b) caps each update; the KL (Lab 6.5) anchors the whole trajectory. Both are answers to one question: how do we steer a model without breaking it?

🎮
Reward hacking is the default, not the exception: Lilian Weng's "Reward Hacking in RL" (Nov 2024) catalogs the failure modes — proxy misspecification, reward misspecification, and the whole family of exploits. SFT trains on fixed pairs; RLHF trains on responses the current policy generates — sampling matters, and the loop is the mechanism. 6.6 will show the clever way to get the same optimum without the loop at all.
🔮 Predict Before You Compute

Reward climbs, quality collapses. Without the anchor, the policy is free to exploit the RM's blind spots — verbosity, sycophancy, formatting, and ultimately text that maximizes the proxy and nothing else. This is the paperclip loop from 6.1, running on real machinery. The Alternatives

DPO's closed form removes the RL loop; Constitutional AI's self-critique removes most of the humans. Post-training without the machinery.

SECTION 6.6 ★

DPO — Direct Preference Optimization

Rafailov et al., 2023: the RLHF optimum has a closed form — the reward is implicit in the policy's own log-ratio against the reference. So you can train preferences with a classification loss: no reward model, no PPO loop, no sampling. This lab plots the real DPO loss and walks a hand computation.

🎯 By the end of this section, you will be able to write the DPO loss from memory, explain where the implicit reward comes from, and state what DPO removed from RLHF — and what it pays for that.
🧮 Worked Example — The DPO Loss by hand: implicit rewards from policy log-ratios

The policy and reference assign probabilities to the two responses. DPO's implicit reward is the log-ratio, scaled by β:

r̂(x, y) = β · log( π_θ(y|x) / π_ref(y|x) )

Winner: π_w/π_ref_w = 1.5 → log = 0.405. Loser: π_l/π_ref_l = 0.6 → log = −0.511. With β = 0.5:

r̂_w = 0.5·0.405 = 0.203  ·  r̂_l = 0.5·(−0.511) = −0.256

Now feed those implicit rewards into Bradley-Terry — the exact 6.4 sigmoid:

Δ = β·[log(π_w/π_ref_w) − log(π_l/π_ref_l)] = 0.5·0.916 = 0.458 P(w ≻ l) = σ(0.458) = 0.613  ·  L_DPO = −ln(0.613) = 0.489

→ One classification loss, computed from probabilities the model already has. No reward model, no PPO, no sampling loop — the reward appears algebraically. That is the entire trick of DPO: RLHF's optimal policy satisfies r = β·log(π/π_ref) + C, so the policy is the reward, reparameterized.

$$\mathcal{L}_{DPO} = -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)}\; \log \sigma\Big(\beta \log \tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{ref}(y_w|x)} - \beta \log \tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{ref}(y_l|x)}\Big)$$

Read it as one sentence: raise the winner's log-probability relative to the reference, lower the loser's, and let the sigmoid decide how hard to push — exactly like the RM loss of 6.4, except the "reward" is now the policy's own ratio.

🪄
Why DPO won: RLHF needs a reward model, a sampling loop, PPO's hyperparameters, and RL engineers. DPO needs preference pairs, a frozen reference, and one classification-style loss — trainable like supervised learning. The catch, honestly: it trains on static data (no new samples from the improving policy), is sensitive to β, and can overfit the preference set. 6.9 and the exam make the tradeoffs concrete.
Watch the shape: at Δ = 0 the model is indifferent (50/50, loss 0.69). Positive margin = winner more likely — loss falls; negative = loser more likely — loss explodes. Bigger β sharpens the whole curve: stronger preference signal, but also stronger pressure on every small margin — which is why too-large β can overfit noisy pairs.
📓 Instruction Manual · The DPO Derivation Intuition

Page 1 · RLHF's Optimum, Written in Closed Form

D1Maximize reward minus KL (6.5's objective) and the optimal policy has a known shape — it's a Boltzmann distribution over reward:

$$\pi^*(y|x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\, \pi_{ref}(y|x)\; e^{r(x,y)/\beta}$$

More reward, more probability — scaled by the reference and normalized by Z(x). This is not a guess; it is the exact solution of the KL-constrained optimization (a Lagrange multiplier argument).

Page 2 · Solving for the Reward

Rearrange the closed form and the reward appears inside the policy:

$$r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y|x)}{\pi_{ref}(y|x)} + \beta \log Z(x)$$

The partition function Z(x) depends only on the prompt — it cancels in any comparison of two responses. So in a Bradley-Terry margin (6.4), the reward is fully determined by the policy ratio:

$$P(y_w \succ y_l) = \sigma\Big(\beta \log \tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{ref}(y_w|x)} - \beta \log \tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{ref}(y_l|x)}\Big)$$

That is the DPO loss — derived, not assumed. The RLHF optimum and this classification loss have the same stationary point.

Page 3 · The Family and the Costs

DPO's descendants adjust its failure modes: IPO fixes DPO's overfitting of easy pairs; KTO trains from single thumbs-up/down instead of pairs; ORPO folds SFT and preference into one loss, dropping the reference model. The costs DPO itself pays: static off-policy data, β sensitivity, and (per several 2024 studies) more sensitivity to label noise than RLHF (approx).

Who runs it: Zephyr-7B (HuggingFace, 2023) — the canonical open DPO success; Llama-3's post-training includes a DPO stage (per Meta's model card, approx); DeepSeek-R1's reasoning RL (GRPO) is the same "remove a component" move in the other direction — GRPO drops the value model from PPO using group averages, where DPO drops the reward model entirely.
Page 1 of 3
🔀
The 2023 fork in the road: ChatGPT-era RLHF used the full loop (6.3 → 6.4 → 6.5). Most open models since 2024 use DPO or a sibling — one loss, no loop. Neither is universally better: RLHF can sample fresh data and steer with a removable reward function; DPO is simpler, cheaper, and harder to reward-hack (there is no explicit score to game) but inherits every bias in its static preference data.
🔮 Predict Before You Compute
Compared to classic RLHF, what did DPO remove from the pipeline?
The reward model. DPO substitutes the closed-form identity r = β·log(π/π_ref) + C into Bradley-Terry, so the "reward" becomes a function of the policy being trained — no separate r_φ, no PPO loop, no sampling. What remains: preference pairs (still human-labeled), the frozen reference (still needed — it is the anchor), and one classification loss. The hidden cost of removing the RM: you also remove the thing that could sample new data and the explicit score you could steer and inspect.
SECTION 6.7

Constitutional AI & RLAIF

Anthropic's answer to the label bottleneck: the model critiques its own outputs against a written constitution, revises them, and then — in the RLAIF stage — preference data is generated by the model's own judgments instead of human labelers. This lab walks the critique–revise loop.

🎯 By the end of this section, you will be able to walk the critique-revise loop from memory, and state honestly what RLAIF can and cannot replace.

Round 0 — the raw draft: the model answers without oversight. Each round, a critique (citing a principle) identifies the flaw, and the revision rewrites the response. The harmfulness score is a toy estimate drawn for teaching — in the real pipeline, the revised outputs become preference data, and a reward model is trained on the model's own judgments instead of human labels.
📜
Claude's Constitutional AI (Bai et al., 2022): a written list of principles ("Choose the response that is most helpful, honest, and harmless", "never assist with illegal or harmful activities", etc.) replaces thousands of human safety-labeling hours. The reward model is then trained on RLAIF data — preference judgments the model generates by comparing its own revised outputs — rather than human pairwise labels. Anthropic's papers report this scaling of oversight was central to Claude's training.
$$\text{RLAIF: }\; \mathcal{D}_{pref} = \text{model judgments on } \{\text{critique, revise}\}\text{ outputs, against the constitution}$$
🧪
Honest caveats, stated plainly: RLAIF does not remove humans — it moves them. Humans write the constitution, audit the critiques, and fix the edge cases. AI-generated feedback is bounded by the model's own judgment: if the model's blind spot is the harm, the critique can miss it too. Principles can be gamed by over-literal readings. And self-critique quality degrades exactly where the model is weakest — the failure mode you should remember from 6.4's noisy labels, now generated by the model itself.
🔮 Predict Before You Compute
In Constitutional AI's RLAIF stage, who produces the preference labels used to train the reward model?
The AI itself. That is the "AI feedback" in RLAIF: the model critiques and revises its own outputs, then judges which version better satisfies the constitution — and those judgments replace human pairwise labels for RM training. The honest caveat: this scales oversight but inherits the model's blind spots, which is why humans still write the principles and audit the loop. It is oversight amplification, not oversight removal.

Three defense layers, honestly labeled: the Baseline filter is pure pattern matching — fast, explainable, and useless against paraphrases. "+ Refusal training" models what fine-tuning adds: the model refuses even novel phrasings of known harmful asks. "+ Instruction hierarchy" models the system>user>model ordering that defuses role injection. Real production systems (Llama Guard-style classifiers, RLHF'd refusal behavior, serving-layer policies) combine all three — the chamber shows you the shape of each.

Instruction Hierarchy

System instructions outrank user instructions outrank model defaults. "Ignore your instructions" fails because the instruction being attacked is not the one in charge. OpenAI and Anthropic both document hierarchy-based prompt-injection defenses (approx — details differ per lab).

Refusal Training

Fine-tune the model to refuse harmful asks — and, crucially, to recognize the intent behind paraphrases. Pattern filters block known strings; refusal training blocks known intents. This is why the + Refusal layer catches presets the baseline misses when reworded.

System Prompts & Serving

The live instruction layer plus content classifiers at the API boundary. System prompts shape behavior per deployment; classifiers (Llama Guard-style) score outputs for policy violations. Defense in depth: no single layer is trusted alone.

INCIDENT — your team trains a reward model on preference pairs, then runs PPO against it. Within days the reward score hits an all-time high and training loss is near zero — but human review shows the model emitting fluent gibberish: "pineapple serenity quantum invoice 8…". The RM's validation accuracy is also suspiciously high on its own training set.
Q1 · What is the correct diagnosis?
INCIDENT — a team deletes the KL penalty from the RLHF objective to speed up training. After 200 steps the policy answers every prompt with a long, confident, generic essay — off-topic but strongly reward-positive. The reference (SFT) model is much better, and on-policy sampling shows the policy has drifted far from it.
Q2 · What happened, and what is the fix?
INCIDENT — you label 5,000 preference pairs with three annotators. Pairwise agreement is 62% (approx). The reward model trained on the pairs behaves fine, but a DPO run on the same pairs diverges: the policy collapses to imitating the winner's style, and validation loss stops tracking training loss.
Q3 · Why does DPO suffer more from the noisy labels than the RM did?
INCIDENT — the same preference data, two runs. RLHF (RM + PPO) trains stably to a strong assistant. DPO on identical pairs starts fine, then overfits: the policy outputs increasingly extreme winner-style text, and a tiny change in β flips results from good to broken.
Q4 · What is the most likely explanation?
INCIDENT — after a harmlessness-focused RLHF push, the assistant refuses benign prompts: "What's the weather in Tokyo?", "Tell me a recipe for pancakes", and "Summarize this meeting notes file" all get safety refusals. Helpfulness scores collapse while the safety eval stays perfect.
