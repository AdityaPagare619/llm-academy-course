The Law — Scaling Curves That Decide Everything

Before the frontier there was a discovery: model loss falls on predictable power laws. Compute became a dial, not a gamble.

SECTION 13.1 ★

The Empirical Law — Loss as a Power Law

Kaplan et al. (2020) found it; Chinchilla (Hoffmann et al., 2022) nailed the constants. Language-model loss follows a smooth, predictable curve as you add parameters and data — the same curve this lab fits live in your browser.

🎯 By the end of this section, you will be able to predict how loss changes when you double a model's parameters, and explain why "Chinchilla-optimal" means parameters and data must grow together.
LAB 13.1 The scaling surface — N and D sliders drive the real Chinchilla fit
1.0B
10B
The Chinchilla fit (nats): L(N,D) = 1.69 + 406.4·N⁻⁰·³⁴ + 410.7·D⁻⁰·²⁸. Move N: loss falls steeply at first, then flattens — the curve of diminishing returns. Move D: the same shape. The insight: both must grow, or you waste one of them.
🧮 Worked Example — Doubling the Model

Setup: N = 1B params, D = 10B tokens. Compute the loss from the fit.

L = 1.69 + 406.4·(10⁹)⁻⁰·³⁴ + 410.7·(10¹⁰)⁻⁰·²⁸ ≈ 1.69 + 0.35 + 0.65 ≈ 2.69 nats

Double N (2B params, same data): the N-term shrinks.

ΔL ≈ 406.4·(2⁻⁰·³⁴ − 1)·(10⁹)⁻⁰·³⁴ ≈ −0.074 nats

Or double D instead: the D-term shrinks.

ΔL ≈ 410.7·(2⁻⁰·²⁸ − 1)·(10¹⁰)⁻⁰·²⁸ ≈ −0.115 nats

Now double both (Chinchilla's move): both terms shrink together — and per unit of compute, that's the most loss you can buy.

→ The exponents (0.34 vs 0.28) tell you data is the harder-to-satisfy term. That asymmetry is the entire reason "more data" became the industry's mantra after 2022.

📓 Instruction Manual · The Scaling Papers — and the derivation that mattered

2020 · Kaplan — "bigger models beat everything"

2020The first scaling paper: loss falls as a power law in parameters, data, and compute. Kaplan's headline: model size matters most — so the field scaled models hard, leaving most models massively under-trained (too many params, too few tokens).

Under-training became the industry's dirty secret: GPT-2 was trained on ~40 tokens per parameter. Kaplan's own curves suggested ~300 would do far better.

2022 · Chinchilla — "data matters as much as size"

2022Hoffmann et al. re-fit the curves with more careful compute accounting and found parameters and data should grow about equally. Their 70B Chinchilla matched the 280B Gopher — same compute, 4× fewer parameters, same loss. The field pivoted overnight: models got smaller, datasets got bigger, and the "~20 tokens per parameter" rule was born.

The derivation (Page 3) is the reason: minimize the loss surface under a compute budget. It is one line of calculus, and it changed the entire industry.

The Derivation — why N ∝ C^0.45

Given L = c + A·N⁻ᵅ + B·D⁻ᵝ and compute C ≈ 6·N·D, minimize L over N with D = C/(6N):

$$\frac{d}{dN}\left[c + A N^{-\alpha} + B (C/6N)^{-\beta}\right] = -\alpha A N^{-\alpha-1} + \beta B (C/6)^{-\beta} N^{\beta-1} = 0$$

Solving: N_opt ∝ C^{β/(α+β)} and D_opt ∝ C^{α/(α+β)}. With Chinchilla's α=0.34, β=0.28: N ∝ C^0.45, D ∝ C^0.55 — both grow, data slightly faster. The "20 tokens per parameter" heuristic is this exponent pair wearing a hat.

2024-2026 · The Refinements

2024Frontier labs publish their own fits (Llama-3, Qwen, DeepSeek) with slightly different constants and steeper data exponents; the "Chinchilla regime" is roughly right, but compute-efficient training deliberately over-trains small models on huge data (the "compute-optimal at serving time" trick: small models trained past the point of diminishing returns because they're cheap to run).

The lesson for your own models: the fit constants are corpus-dependent — but the SHAPE of the law is robust. Predict with it, verify against it, and never trust a single number.

🔮 Predict Before You Compute
A lab doubles its training compute (C × 2). To stay Chinchilla-optimal, how should it split the new compute between N and D?
Grow both. The derivation on Page 3 is exact: with α=0.34 and β=0.28, the optimal split is N ∝ C^0.45 and D ∝ C^0.55. All-params wastes the steep part of the data term; all-data wastes the model term. The industry's "20 tokens per parameter" is this pair in disguise.
SECTION 13.2

The IsoFLOP Frontier — Squeezing Every FLOP

For a fixed compute budget, the N/D split is a choice — and the loss surface has a valley. The IsoFLOP curves are slices through that valley: one U-shape per budget, one optimal point per U.

🎯 By the end of this section, you will be able to read an IsoFLOP curve, find the compute-optimal point by eye, and explain the cost of deviating from it.
LAB 13.2 IsoFLOP curves — one compute budget, all possible N/D splits
The U-shape: each curve holds compute fixed (C ≈ 6ND) and varies the split. Too few params → the model can't learn (left wall). Too many → the data term starves (right wall). The valley is the Chinchilla point. Bigger budgets → deeper valleys, lower loss, and the optimum slides right.
📐
Reading the frontier: every frontier lab publishes roughly the same picture — the same U-shapes, different constants. When a model "beats" another at the same compute, what it really did was sit closer to the valley. The frontier is not a hardware race; it is a curve-fitting race with FLOPs as the currency.
🔮 Predict Before You Compute
Two teams use the same compute budget (C ≈ 9×10^23 FLOPs). Team A trains a 500B model on 300B tokens; Team B trains a 100B model on 1.5T tokens. What does the IsoFLOP picture predict?
Team B wins. Team A is the classic under-trained regime: 300B tokens on 500B params is ~0.6 tokens per parameter — far below Chinchilla's ~20. On the IsoFLOP curve, A sits high on the right wall while B sits in the valley. Same compute, very different loss. This exact mistake fueled the 2022 data panic.
Part II

The Machines — Reasoning and State

The frontier's two biggest shifts since 2024: spending compute at inference time, and replacing attention with state.

SECTION 13.3 ★

The Reasoning Frontier — Compute at Inference Time

Scaling laws govern training. The 2024-2026 revolution added a second axis: test-time compute. Give the model more tokens to think — search, verify, backtrack — and its accuracy climbs on hard problems, sometimes steeply. o1 and o3 (OpenAI), DeepSeek-R1, Gemini 2.5, and Claude's "thinking" modes all ride this curve.

🎯 By the end of this section, you will be able to explain why test-time compute helps on hard problems and hurts on trivia, and read the tradeoff honestly.
LAB 13.3 The reasoning frontier — accuracy vs thinking budget
Hard problems: accuracy climbs as you grant more thinking tokens — steeply at first, then saturating (a log-like curve; shape reported for o1-class models, labeled approx). Trivia: the curve is flat — extra thought is wasted latency. The engineering skill is knowing which curve you're on.
📓 Instruction Manual · The Thinking Timeline

2022 · Chain-of-Thought — the first nudge

2022Wei et al. discover that "think step by step" unlocks multi-step reasoning that direct answer-generation fumbles. The model's own tokens become a scratchpad. Modest gains, no training change.

The seed: the model already reasons internally — prompting just externalizes it. Test-time compute had entered the room.

2024 · o1 — RL on reasoning traces

2024OpenAI trains o1 with reinforcement learning on long reasoning traces: the model learns to search — backtrack, reconsider, verify — inside its hidden "thinking" tokens. On hard math/coding benchmarks the gain is a step-change, not a nudge. The industry races to replicate: DeepSeek-R1 (2025, open weights), Gemini 2.5, Claude thinking modes.

The deep result: reasoning is a trainable skill, not a prompt trick. RL found strategies humans never wrote down.

2025-2026 · The economics of thinking

2025Thinking costs tokens — and money. R1-style models think on easy questions too, unless the system decides when to think. The frontier's practical skill is the adaptive switch: a cheap model for trivia, a thinking model for hard math, and a router deciding which. The flat curve in the lab is the reason.

SECTION 13.4

State Space Models — Attention's Linear-Time Rival

Attention's cost is O(n²) in sequence length — the wall Module 11's FlashAttention climbed. Mamba (Gu & Dao, 2023-2024) takes a different road: a recurrent state that compresses history to a fixed-size vector, scanning the sequence in O(n).

🎯 By the end of this section, you will be able to run a state-space recurrence by hand, and state exactly what the O(n) trick gives up.
LAB 13.4 The scan — a real recurrence, step by step
The recurrence: h_t = A·h_{t−1} + B·x_t, y_t = C·h_t — one state vector, updated per token, no pairwise matrix. Attention compares every pair; the SSM compresses everything into one vector. That compression IS the trade: recall of exact details fades as the state overwrites itself.
🧮 Worked Example — The Scan by Hand (state dim 2, A = 0.8)

Token 1 · x = [1, 0]: h₁ = 0.8·[0,0] + [1,0] = [1, 0]. Output y₁ = C·h₁ (C = [1, 0]).

h₁ = [1.00, 0.00] → y₁ = 1.00

Token 2 · x = [0, 1]: the state mixes the new input with the decaying old one.

h₂ = 0.8·[1,0] + [0,1] = [0.80, 1.00] → y₂ = 0.80

Token 3 · x = [0, 0]: no input — the state just decays.

h₃ = 0.8·[0.8,1] + 0 = [0.64, 0.80] → y₃ = 0.64

The lesson: token 1's influence halves every step (0.8^k). Attention would remember it exactly at any distance; the SSM remembers it approximately, forever. That is the price of O(n).

→ Mamba-2 and hybrid models (Jamba, Zamba) keep attention where exact recall matters and SSM where compression is fine.

🔮 Predict Before You Compute
Which task would an SSM-based model struggle with most, compared to a transformer?
Exact long-range recall. The state is a fixed-size summary; a needle 500 tokens back has been overwritten many times. This is the measured weakness of pure SSMs on retrieval-heavy tasks — and the exact reason the 2024-2026 pattern is hybrid: SSM for the bulk, attention (or retrieval) for the needles.

The Scale — Big Models, Longer Memory

How the frontier fits trillions of parameters into finite hardware — and how it remembers millions of tokens.

SECTION 13.5

MoE at Frontier Scale — 671B Total, 37B Active

Module 03 taught you the router. Now see it at scale: DeepSeek-V3 (671B total parameters, 37B active per token), Mixtral 8×7B, GPT-4-class systems. The trick: most of the brain sleeps on every token — only a few experts wake up.

🎯 By the end of this section, you will be able to compute active vs total parameters, explain the load-balancing problem, and state why MoE trains cheap but serves tricky.
LAB 13.5 The routing dashboard — real gating, live experts
Live gating: each token's hidden state multiplies a real router matrix; softmax picks the top experts. Watch the active-parameter counter: 3 of 6 experts awake ≈ half the compute, all the knowledge. Flip to LOAD BALANCE to see the failure mode: when one expert hoards tokens, the others starve — and the load-balancing loss exists to stop it.
📓 Instruction Manual · The MoE Math

Page 1 · Total vs Active

A router per layer picks the top-k experts for each token. Total parameters = all experts' weights (the knowledge). Active parameters = the k experts actually run (the compute). DeepSeek-V3: 671B total, 37B active — 18× knowledge per FLOP.

$$\text{FLOPs per token} \propto \text{active}, \qquad \text{memory} \propto \text{total}$$
This is why MoE trains cheaper per FLOP than dense — but serving still loads ALL experts into VRAM (total!), so a 671B MoE needs multi-GPU serving even though each token touches 37B.

Page 2 · The Load-Balancing Loss

Without a penalty, the router collapses: one expert learns to win most tokens, the others atrophy. The fix is an auxiliary loss that rewards even usage (Shazeer 2017; the Switch Transformer's load-balancing term, 2021).

$$\mathcal{L}_{aux} = \alpha \cdot N \cdot \sum_{i=1}^{E} f_i \cdot p_i$$

f_i = fraction of tokens routed to expert i, p_i = the router's average probability for i. The product is small only when routing matches probability — i.e., when experts are used proportionally.

Page 3 · The 2024-2026 Record

2024Mixtral 8×7B proves open-weight MoE can match dense quality at 3× lower inference FLOPs. 2025DeepSeek-V3: 671B total / 37B active with fine-grained experts (256) and shared experts — the largest open frontier model of its era; R1 adds the reasoning layer on top. The lesson: the architecture you built in Module 03, scaled 10,000×.

🔮 Predict Before You Compute
You delete the load-balancing loss from a 100-expert MoE and train for a long time. What do you observe?
Collapse. Routing is a competition; without the balancing term, the winners take all. This is a measured failure mode on real MoE training runs — the auxiliary loss exists because the collapse was observed, not theorized. SECTION 13.6

The Context Frontier — The 1M-Token Era

GPT-4 launched at 8K tokens. By 2026, frontier models advertise 200K, 1M, even 2M. This section is the honest math of what context length actually costs — attention, memory, and accuracy.

🎯 By the end of this section, you will be able to compute the real cost of doubling context, and explain why "supports 1M tokens" and "uses 1M tokens well" are different claims.
LAB 13.6 The context cost meter — slide the window, watch the bills
128K
Three bills, one slider: attention FLOPs grow O(T²) (quadratic — the wall), KV cache memory grows O(T) linearly (the 11.2 formula), and "effective recall" — how much the model actually uses — grows sublinearly (real measured pattern, labeled approx). The frontier's tricks (RoPE scaling, sliding windows, retrieval) bend each bill differently.
🧯
The honest read of "1M context": the model can accept 1M tokens (the cache fits) — but attention quality on needles past ~100K degrades (the "needle-in-haystack" saturation), and cost per query explodes. The marketing number is a memory claim, not an accuracy claim. When you deploy, you measure the curve you saw in Lab 13.3 — for context, not thinking.
🔮 Predict Before You Compute
You double a model's context from 64K to 128K. How does the attention cost change?
It quadruples. FlashAttention (Module 11) made the memory linear, but the pairwise compute is still O(T²) — 128K² = 4× 64K². This is why every "long context" system quietly uses sparsity, sliding windows, or retrieval: the quadratic bill is a law, not a bug.

SECTION 13.8

The Road Ahead — What the Laws Don't Cover

The scaling laws are real — and they are not the whole story. The honest map of the open frontier, as of 2026.

🎯 By the end of this section, you will be able to name the open problems that scaling alone cannot solve — and pick the one you want to attack.

The Data Ceiling

High-quality text is finite. Labs recycle corpora, synthesize data, and mine reasoning traces — but the power law wants ever-more tokens. The honest question: does the curve bend when the data well runs dry? Frontier labs are betting both ways.

Evaluation Collapse

Benchmarks saturate (Module 10). Contamination spreads. The field measures itself with instruments that no longer discriminate. Building honest evals is one of the highest-leverage skills in the entire industry — and it is yours from Module 10.

The Alignment Gap

Scaling made models capable; alignment (Module 06) makes them safe. But capability outruns control: red-teaming, interpretability (Module 12), and mechanistic guarantees are all still catching up. Every frontier lab's hardest problem is listed in a module you've now completed.

Agent Reliability

Agents (Module 09) hold tools and take actions — and still fail in ways that are hard to predict. Long-horizon reliability, memory, and self-correction are open problems with real jobs behind them.

Architecture's Next Act

SSMs, MoE, long-context tricks (13.4-13.6) are refinements of the transformer, not replacements. The next architecture — if it comes — must beat attention on its own terms. The people who build it will be the people who understand the current one deeply.

The Interpretability Promise

If we can truly read models (Module 12), we can certify them — guarantee behaviors instead of hope for them. It is the deepest unsolved problem on this board, and the one most likely to be solved by someone willing to build the next lens.
