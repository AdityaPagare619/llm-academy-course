The Single Brick

SECTION 0.1

The Perceptron: Where It All Began

Before neural networks, before deep learning, before GPT — there was a single function: multiply, sum, threshold. Frank Rosenblatt's Perceptron (1958) was the first machine that could learn from data. Its failure on one tiny problem froze AI for fifteen years. Its repair — adding layers — is why you're reading this course.

🎯 By the end of this section, you will understand why a single neuron can solve AND and OR but not XOR — and why that limitation nearly killed artificial intelligence.
FOUNDRY NOTEBOOK · THE BIRTH OF LEARNING MACHINES

🏗️ The Machine That Could Learn

1958

Frank Rosenblatt, a psychologist at Cornell, builds the Mark I Perceptron — a room-sized machine with 400 photocells connected by wires to a grid of potentiometers (adjustable resistors). Each potentiometer is a weight. The machine learns to recognize simple shapes by adjusting these weights based on whether it got the answer right or wrong.

The New York Times headline reads: "Electronic Brain Teaches Itself." The Navy, which funded it, declares it the beginning of machines that "will be able to walk, talk, see, write, reproduce itself and be conscious of its existence."

The core idea is radical and correct: a machine that improves by looking at its own mistakes. Every AI system you've ever used descends from this exact idea.

📐 The Math — Beautifully Simple

The Perceptron computes one thing:

$$\text{output} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } \sum_{i} w_i x_i \geq \theta \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

Multiply each input by its weight. Sum them up. If the sum meets or exceeds a threshold θ, fire (output 1). Otherwise, stay silent (output 0). That's the entire algorithm.

This is a linear decision boundary — a straight line (or hyperplane in higher dimensions) that divides the input space into "yes" and "no." Any problem that can be separated by a straight line, the Perceptron can learn to solve.

The Perceptron is a single LEGO brick. It can be flat. It can be long. But it can only be straight. To make curves, you need to stack.

❄️ The Crash — and the Fifteen-Year Winter

1969

Marvin Minsky and Seymour Papert publish Perceptrons — a rigorous mathematical proof that single-layer perceptrons cannot compute XOR (exclusive-or). XOR is trivial for any programmer: "output 1 when exactly one input is 1." But no single straight line can separate [0,0],[1,1] from [0,1],[1,0] on a 2D plane.

The proof is technically limited — it only applies to single-layer networks, not multi-layer ones. But the damage is total. Funding evaporates. Researchers flee the field. The first AI Winter begins and lasts until 1986.

1986

Rumelhart, Hinton, and Williams publish "Learning Representations by Back-propagating Errors" in Nature. They prove that multi-layer networks + backpropagation can learn XOR — and anything else. The solution to Minsky's critique was always just one hidden layer away. You'll build that proof yourself in Section 0.4.

THE LINEAR BOUNDARY

A perceptron divides its input space with a straight line (2D), plane (3D), or hyperplane (nD). Everything on one side → class 0. Everything on the other → class 1. Problems that can be split this way are called linearly separable. AND, OR, NOT: linearly separable. XOR: not.

THE PERCEPTRON LEARNING RULE

Rosenblatt's update rule: if the output is wrong, nudge each weight toward the correct answer: w_i ← w_i + η(y - ŷ)x_i. This is the ancestor of gradient descent — simpler (no derivatives), but only works for linearly separable problems. And it provably converges if a solution exists.

WHY THIS MATTERS FOR LLMS

Every neuron in GPT-4 is a perceptron with a smooth activation function instead of a hard threshold. The step function → sigmoid → ReLU evolution (Section 0.2) replaces the binary "fire or don't" with "fire this much." That smooth version is differentiable, which is what makes backpropagation possible.

SECTION 0.2

The Modern Neuron: A Tiny Function That Learns

The perceptron's hard threshold is a cliff: on or off. The modern neuron replaces it with a smooth function — sigmoid, tanh, or ReLU — that outputs a gradient, not just a binary. That smoothness is what makes backpropagation (Section 0.7) possible. GPT-4 is ~1.8 trillion of these. Choose your activation and watch the same neuron behave completely differently.

🎯 By the end of this section, you will be able to compute a neuron's output by hand and explain why ReLU unlocked deep learning.
$$y = \sigma\left(\sum_{i} w_i x_i + b\right) = \sigma(w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + b)$$

The weights w_i control how much each input matters. The bias b shifts the whole thing. The function σ (sigmoid, tanh, or ReLU) adds nonlinearity — without it, any number of layers collapses into a single matrix multiply.

💡
Key insight: Without the nonlinearity, a 100-layer network is mathematically identical to a 1-layer network. The activation function is what gives depth its power.
🧱
The Lego connection: A neuron is one Lego stud. It has input tubes (weights) bringing signal in, and it emits one output signal. Connect thousands of studs in layers and you get a network. Section 0.2 shows this as a perfect 2D structure.
🧪
The activation zoo: sigmoid squashes to (0,1) but saturates and kills gradients. tanh squashes to (−1,1) — better, but still saturates. ReLU just passes positive values through — simple, fast, and the reason deep nets train at all. Swish/SiLU (x·σ(x)) and GELU are the modern smooth variants used in LLMs. Try them all below.

SECTION 0.3

Why Nonlinearity Is Everything

You can stack 100 linear layers and the result is… one linear layer. That's not a simplification — it's a mathematical fact called the linear collapse theorem. Nonlinear activations break the collapse. Without them, neural networks are glorified linear regression. With them, they can approximate any function in existence.

🎯 By the end of this section, you will be able to prove that N stacked linear layers = 1 linear layer — and demonstrate why one ReLU changes everything.
FOUNDRY NOTEBOOK · THE LINEAR COLLAPSE THEOREM

📐 The Proof That Changes Everything

Consider two linear layers applied in sequence:

$$y = W_2(W_1 x) = (W_2 W_1) x = W' x$$

Matrix multiplication is associative. No matter how many matrices you chain — $W_3 W_2 W_1 x$ = $(W_3 W_2 W_1) x$ = $W' x$ — the result is a single matrix $W'$ applied to $x$. One multiplication. One layer. A "deep" linear network is a shallow linear network in disguise.

This is why the perceptron died. No matter how many you chain, without nonlinearity, the network can only draw straight lines. The activation function — sigmoid, tanh, ReLU — is the brick that breaks the line into curves.

🧱 The LEGO Interpretation

Think of each linear layer as a flat LEGO plate. No matter how many flat plates you stack, the result is still flat. But add one angled brick (a nonlinearity), and suddenly you can build ramps, arches, towers.

Every modern LLM uses GELU or SiLU (Swish) — smooth curves instead of ReLU's sharp corner. But the principle is identical: without that nonlinearity between layers, the 96-layer GPT-4 transformer would collapse into a single linear transformation. It would be the world's most expensive linear regression.

THE COLLAPSE THEOREM

For any chain of linear transformations $W_n \cdots W_2 W_1$, the result is a single linear transformation $W'$. This is matrix associativity. A "100-layer linear network" has the same expressive power as a single layer. Depth without nonlinearity is free parameters wasted.

WHY RELU DOMINATES

ReLU(x) = max(0, x). Its gradient is 1 for x > 0, 0 for x < 0. Unlike sigmoid/tanh, it doesn't squash large values — gradients flow freely through positive regions. This is why networks went from 2-3 layers (1990s) to 100+ layers (2010s).

MODERN LLMS: GELU & SWISH

GPT-2+ uses GELU: $x \cdot \Phi(x)$ where $\Phi$ is the Gaussian CDF. Llama uses SiLU (Swish): $x \cdot \sigma(x)$. Both are smooth approximations of ReLU that allow small negative gradients. The smoothness helps optimizer convergence.

The Structure (2-4-1)

2 input neurons (the XOR inputs), 4 hidden neurons (each learns a different linear boundary), 1 output neuron (combines them). The hidden layer's nonlinearity lets the network curve its decision boundary — something a single neuron can't do. This exact structure appears as the FFN inside every transformer block (Module 03).

The Forward Pass

Signal flows left→right. Each hidden neuron computes h = \tanh(w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + b). The output computes \hat{y} = \sigma(\sum w_i h_i + b). Watch each neuron light up as its activation is computed — the "glow" intensity is the activation value.

The Backward Pass

The loss at the output pulses backward through the tubes. Each weight gets a gradient: ∂L/∂w = "how much did this connection contribute to the error?" Then every weight steps in the opposite direction — the tubes thicken or thin. That is learning. Section 0.5 shows the chain rule node by node.

SECTION 0.5

Loss Functions: Measuring "How Wrong" Across a Landscape

A loss function turns "the model's prediction" into a single number: how wrong it is. Training = minimizing this number. But different loss functions create different landscapes — and the landscape's shape determines how hard the optimization is. Explore MSE and Cross-Entropy on a live 2D loss surface.

🎯 By the end of this section, you will be able to explain why cross-entropy creates steeper gradients than MSE when the model is confident and wrong.
0.5 · LOSS LANDSCAPE
Drag the red point across the landscape. The color shows the loss surface (teal valleys = low loss, coral peaks = high loss). This is a single neuron's two weights on a tiny dataset. The ball in Section 0.4 will roll down this exact landscape.
$$L_{MSE} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2 \qquad L_{CE} = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} y_i \log(\hat{y}_i)$$

Mean Squared Error (left): for regression. Punishes large errors quadratically — an error of 2 costs 4× an error of 1. Creates a smooth bowl landscape.

Cross-Entropy (right): for classification. This is what LLMs minimize — the negative log-likelihood of the correct next token. When the model assigns probability p to the right answer, loss = −log(p). Creates a sharper, more directional landscape.

🎯
Connection to LLMs: When GPT predicts the next word, it's doing classification over ~50,000 possible tokens. Cross-entropy loss = −log(probability it gave the actual next word). Perplexity = eloss. The model that compresses text best is, provably, the best next-token predictor.
🏔️
Why the landscape matters: optimization is navigation. A smooth, convex landscape (MSE on linear regression) has one valley — easy. A non-convex landscape (deep networks) has thousands of valleys, saddle points, and plateaus — this is why training is hard and why the optimizer (Section 0.9) matters so much.
🔮 PREDICTION MOMENT · Think before you switch…
A model outputs probability 0.01 for the correct answer. Which loss function produces a LARGER gradient signal to fix this mistake?
Cross-Entropy wins by a landslide. MSE gives gradient ≈ 2×0.99 = 1.98. Cross-entropy gives gradient 1/0.01 = 100. That's 50× stronger. This is why every classifier (and every LLM) uses cross-entropy: it screams at the model when it's confidently wrong, which is exactly when the strongest correction signal is needed.
SECTION 0.6

Gradient Descent: Rolling Downhill Blindfolded

The gradient ∇L points uphill (direction of steepest increase). We go the opposite way: downhill. The learning rate controls step size. Too high and the ball flies off the landscape; too low and it crawls forever. Watch the ball roll on the exact landscape from Section 0.5.

🎯 By the end of this section, you will be able to predict whether a given learning rate will converge, oscillate, or diverge — before running the simulation.
$$w \leftarrow w - \eta \cdot \frac{\partial L}{\partial w}$$

η (eta) is the learning rate — the single most important hyperparameter in all of deep learning. It decides whether training converges, diverges, or crawls.

The Goldilocks Problem

LR too high: the ball bounces past the minimum and diverges to infinity (watch it with LR=1.5).
LR too low: the ball crawls; training takes forever (try LR=0.01).
LR just right: smooth descent into the valley (~0.3 here).

⚠️
Real-world note: Modern LLMs use warmup + cosine decay schedules, not a fixed LR. You'll see this in Module 05. But the core idea is identical: follow the negative gradient. The ball here is doing exactly what a trillion-parameter model does — just in 2 dimensions instead of a trillion.

SECTION 0.7 ★ THE KEYSTONE

Backpropagation: The Chain Rule, Visualized Node by Node

This is the algorithm that makes all of deep learning work. It's just the chain rule from calculus, applied recursively through a computation graph. Build the graph below, then run forward and backward passes to see every intermediate value and gradient.

🎯 By the end of this section, you will be able to compute the gradient at any node in a computation graph using the chain rule — by hand.
🧠
The whole idea: For every operation, we know its local derivative. The chain rule says: gradient at input = gradient at output × local derivative. Backprop walks the graph in reverse, multiplying as it goes. That's it — that's the entire algorithm.
Computation Graph — nodes show value (top) and gradient (bottom)
Click Forward Pass to compute values flowing left→right. Then Backward Pass to compute gradients flowing right→left via the chain rule. Watch each node update.

The Chain Rule, Step by Step

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial a} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial out} \cdot \frac{\partial out}{\partial d} \cdot \frac{\partial d}{\partial a}$$

For d = a · b: the local derivatives are ∂d/∂a = b and ∂d/∂b = a. So gradient flowing into d gets multiplied by b to reach a, and by a to reach b.

// Karpathy's micrograd — the entire backward pass
class Value {
  constructor(data, children=[]) {
    this.data = data; this.grad = 0;
  }
  mul(other) {
    const out = new Value(this.data * other.data, [this, other]);
    out._backward = () => {
      this.grad += other.data * out.grad; // ∂/∂this
      other.grad += this.data * out.grad; // ∂/∂other
    };
    return out;
  }
}
🔮 PREDICTION MOMENT · Before you run backward…
In the graph f = (a + b) × c, the forward output is f = 6 and ∂f/∂f = 1. What is the gradient ∂f/∂a?
It equals c. Let s = a + b, so f = s × c. By chain rule: ∂f/∂a = ∂f/∂s × ∂s/∂a. Since ∂f/∂s = c and ∂s/∂a = 1, we get ∂f/∂a = c. The gradient at any node = upstream gradient × local derivative. That's the entire algorithm.

SECTION 0.9

Initialization: The First Weights Decide Everything

Start with weights too large and signals explode layer by layer. Too small and they vanish. The right initialization keeps variance ≈ 1 through every layer — and it's the difference between a network that learns in seconds and one that never learns at all.

🎯 By the end of this section, you will be able to derive why Var(W) = 2/n_in is the correct formula for ReLU networks.
0.9 · INIT EXPLORER
Watch activation variance (teal) and gradient variance (coral) through 10 layers. Good init keeps both flat ≈ 1. Bad init explodes or vanishes exponentially. All-zeros is the special failure: every neuron in a layer becomes identical, forever — they can never differentiate.
$$\text{Var}(W) = \frac{2}{n_{in} + n_{out}} \;\; \text{(Xavier)} \qquad \text{Var}(W) = \frac{2}{n_{in}} \;\; \text{(He)}$$

Xavier/Glorot: balances variance between fan-in and fan-out — designed for tanh/sigmoid where activations are centered. He/Kaiming: doubles the variance — designed for ReLU, which kills half the activations (the negative half), so it needs 2× to compensate.

🎲
Why zeros are catastrophic: if all weights start identical, every neuron in a layer computes the same output and receives the same gradient. They update identically, forever. The layer effectively has ONE neuron, no matter how wide it is. This is the "symmetry breaking" problem — randomness isn't a nicety, it's a requirement.
🔗
Connection to LLMs: transformers use a variant scaled by depth (init ∝ 1/√(2·layers)) so the residual stream doesn't grow with depth. Our MicroGPT uses Xavier on Linear layers and std=0.02 normal on embeddings — you can verify this in train_micro_gpt_tinystories.py.
🔮 PREDICTION MOMENT · Before you pick an init strategy…
A 10-layer ReLU network uses weights drawn from N(0, σ=5.0). After a forward pass, what happens to the activation magnitudes?
They explode. With σ=5.0 and n_in neurons per layer, variance grows by a factor of ~n_in×σ² per layer. After 10 layers, activations are ~10²⁰ — numerical infinity. He initialization (σ = √(2/n_in)) keeps variance ≈1 at every layer. Try it in the demo above.

Why XOR Is the Classic Test

No single straight line separates (0,1) and (1,0) from (0,0) and (1,1). It was the proof in 1969 (Minsky & Papert's Perceptrons) that single-layer networks are limited — the finding that stalled AI for a decade, until multi-layer networks + backpropagation solved it. You just solved it yourself in Section 0.2.

Why Depth Matters for Spirals

XOR needs 2 hidden neurons; circles need ~4; a two-armed spiral needs 6+ and deeper nonlinearity. Each hidden neuron contributes one "bend" to the decision boundary. This is exactly why LLMs need 100+ layers: language is far more curved than a spiral.

The Universal Approximation Catch

In theory, one hidden layer with enough neurons can approximate ANY function (Cybenko 1989, Hornik 1991). In practice, the required width is astronomically large. Depth is the efficiency hack: deep nets approximate complex functions with exponentially fewer neurons. That's the entire reason "deep" learning exists.

SECTION 0.10

Why Deep Networks Are Hard to Train

Stack 20 layers and... nothing learns. Gradients vanish to zero or explode to infinity. This nearly killed deep learning twice. Here's the diagnosis and the cure — the same cures that make 100-layer transformers possible.

🎯 By the end of this section, you will be able to predict whether gradients will vanish or explode just by looking at the init and activation function.
0.10 · ACTIVATION STATS
■ Activation std per layer ■ Gradient std per layer

The Diagnosis

Vanishing gradients: with tanh and bad init, each layer shrinks the gradient. After 10 layers: 0.510 ≈ 0.001. The first layers learn ~1000× slower than the last.

Exploding gradients: with too-large weights, each layer amplifies. After 10 layers: 210 = 1024. Loss becomes NaN.

💊
The cures (all in LLMs):
1. Careful init (Xavier/He): keep variance ≈ 1 per layer
2. ReLU-family: no saturation for positive values
3. Normalization (BatchNorm/LayerNorm/RMSNorm): re-center per layer
4. Residual connections: gradient highway past layers
$$\text{Var}(W) = \frac{2}{n_{in} + n_{out}} \quad \text{(Xavier)}$$

Transformers use RMSNorm + residuals + careful init. That's why 100+ layer models can train at all. You'll meet these again in Module 03.

🔮 PREDICTION MOMENT · Before you add more layers…
You stack 20 layers of tanh activations with no special initialization. What happens to the gradient of the first layer's weights?
Gradients vanish. Tanh's maximum derivative is 1.0 (at z=0), and it's <1 everywhere else. After 20 layers, the gradient ≈ (∂tanh/∂z)²⁰ ≈ 0. The first layer barely learns. This is the vanishing gradient problem that nearly killed deep learning — solved by ReLU (whose derivative is exactly 1 for z>0), residual connections (skip the multiplication), and normalization layers.
SECTION 0.13 ★ STABILIZATION

Normalization: LayerNorm & RMSNorm

As activations pass through dozens of layers, their scale drifts and explodes or collapses. Normalization reshapes vector distributions to mean 0 and variance 1 across feature dimensions. Modern LLMs like LLaMA and Mistral rely heavily on RMSNorm for fast, stable training.

🎯 By the end of this section, you will understand why LLMs prefer LayerNorm/RMSNorm over BatchNorm, and how RMSNorm simplifies normalization by ignoring the mean.
FOUNDRY NOTEBOOK · LAYERNORM & RMSNORM

⚖️ Why Normalize Across Features?

In vision (CNNs), BatchNorm normalizes across the batch dimension. But in LLMs, batch sizes vary and sequence lengths change. LayerNorm normalizes across the feature/embedding dimension for each token independently.

$$\bar{x} = \frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^d x_i, \quad \sigma^2 = \frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^d (x_i - \bar{x})^2$$
$$\text{LN}(x) = \frac{x - \bar{x}}{\sqrt{\sigma^2 + \epsilon}} \odot \gamma + \beta$$

This guarantees that no matter how deep the network gets, the vector passed into each attention layer has unit variance.

⚡ RMSNorm: The Speed Optimization

Zhang & Sennrich (2019) discovered that LayerNorm's success comes from scaling property, not mean-centering! RMSNorm (Root Mean Square Normalization) discards mean-centering completely:

$$\text{RMS}(x) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^d x_i^2 + \epsilon}$$
$$\text{RMSNorm}(x) = \frac{x}{\text{RMS}(x)} \odot \gamma$$
RMSNorm requires ~10-50% less computation per layer because it eliminates calculating $\bar{x}$ and subtracting it. LLaMA, Mistral, and Gemma all use RMSNorm.
Page 1 of 2
🔮 PREDICTION MOMENT · Think before you normalize…
Why is BatchNorm unsuitable for autoregressive text generation (inference with batch size = 1)?
Correct! BatchNorm computes statistics across all samples in a mini-batch. At inference time (e.g., generating text token-by-token for a single user), batch size is 1, so batch statistics are noisy or nonexistent. LayerNorm/RMSNorm operate entirely within a single token vector ($d$-dimensional embedding), making them completely independent of batch size!

SECTION 0.14 ★ REGULARIZATION

Regularization: Dropout & Weight Decay

Overparameterized neural networks tend to memorize training data instead of generalizing. Regularization techniques penalize complexity and force neurons to learn robust features. Here we explore L2 Weight Decay and Inverted Dropout.

🎯 By the end of this section, you will be able to explain how Dropout prevents co-adaptation of features and why scaling by 1/(1-p) is necessary during training.
🔮 PREDICTION MOMENT · Think before dropping studs…
If you randomly drop 20% of activations (p = 0.2) during training, what must you do to the remaining 80% to keep the expected sum constant?
Multiply by 1/(1-p) = 1.25! This is called Inverted Dropout. By scaling by $1/(1-p)$ during training, the expected activation magnitude remains identical during test time without requiring any changes or scaling when evaluating the model!

SECTION 0.15 ★ THE GRADIENT HIGHWAY

Residual Connections: Skipping Layers to Train Deep Nets

Before He et al. (2015) introduced Residual Networks (ResNets), training networks deeper than 20 layers was nearly impossible due to vanishing gradients. By adding a simple identity shortcut $y = x + F(x)$, gradients flow uninterrupted through 100+ layers.

🎯 By the end of this section, you will see why adding x directly to F(x) turns backpropagation into an unobstructed gradient highway.
FOUNDRY NOTEBOOK · RESIDUAL SHORTCUTS

🛣️ The Gradient Highway Derivative

Consider a standard residual block:

$$y = x + F(x)$$

When we compute the derivative with respect to input $x$:

$$\frac{\partial y}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(x) + \frac{\partial F(x)}{\partial x} = \mathbf{1} + F'(x)$$
Notice the term +1! Even if $F'(x)$ vanishes completely to 0 (e.g. saturated activations), the gradient signal is guaranteed to pass backwards with a multiplier of at least 1!

🏰 Residual Blocks in Transformers

Every Transformer block consists of two residual sub-layers:

$$x^{(1)} = x + \text{Attention}(\text{Norm}(x))$$
$$x^{(2)} = x^{(1)} + \text{MLP}(\text{Norm}(x^{(1)}))$$

The "main trunk" or residual stream carries information directly from token embeddings all the way to the final unembedding layer, while each sub-layer only calculates small additive updates ($\Delta x$).

The Optimizer Race: SGD vs Momentum vs Adam

Every weight steps by gradient × learning rate — but how you compute that step changes everything. Three optimizers start at the same point on the same loss ravine. Watch them race to the minimum — and see why Adam's per-parameter learning rates win almost every time in deep learning.

SGD Momentum Adam
0.10
Adam (teal) should reach the minimum fastest on this ravine — it adapts each parameter's step size. SGD (coral) zigzags in narrow valleys; Momentum (gold) smooths the path. This is a simplified preview of Module 05's full optimizer deep-dive with Adam bias-correction and gradient clipping.
🔮 PREDICTION MOMENT · Before you start the race…
A loss surface is a steep "ravine" (very steep in y, very flat in x). Which optimizer will cross the ravine to the minimum in fewest steps?
Adam wins. SGD zigzags back and forth across the steep walls of the ravine, making almost zero progress along the floor. Momentum helps by building velocity forward, but Adam tracks both momentum (first moment $m$) and variance (second moment $v$). It divides by $\sqrt{v}$, automatically shrinking steps in the steep direction and expanding steps in the flat direction.

SGD

$$w \leftarrow w - \eta \, g_t$$

Follows the raw gradient every step. Simple, but zigzags in narrow valleys (the "ravine problem") and crawls on plateaus.

Momentum

$$v \leftarrow \beta v + g_t;\quad w \leftarrow w - \eta v$$

Accumulates a velocity. Smooths zigzag, accelerates through consistent directions. β ≈ 0.9. Like a ball with mass rolling downhill.

Adam

$$m=\beta_1 m+(1-\beta_1)g;\ v=\beta_2 v+(1-\beta_2)g^2;\ w \leftarrow w - \eta \frac{\hat m}{\sqrt{\hat v}+\epsilon}$$

Tracks gradient mean (m) and variance (v). Each parameter gets its own effective step size. This is what trains every modern LLM (as AdamW, with decoupled weight decay).
