📓 Instruction Manual · Reading a Transformer Block

Page 1 · The Sublayer Sandwich

'17The 2017 paper Attention Is All You Need defined the block as a sandwich with two layers of filling: multi-head attention (tokens exchange information) and a position-wise feed-forward network (each token "thinks" alone). Both are wrapped in residual connections and surrounded by normalization:

$$x \;\to\; \text{Norm} \;\to\; \text{Attention} \;\to\; x + \text{Attn}(x) \;\to\; \text{Norm} \;\to\; \text{MLP} \;\to\; x + \text{MLP}(x)$$

That is the whole block. GPT-2 stacks 12 of them, GPT-3 stacks 96, Llama-3 405B stacks 126. Same sandwich, repeated.

Page 2 · The Residual Wrappers

Each sublayer is wrapped in a "+": the output is added to the input, never replacing it.

$$x' = x + \text{Attn}(\text{Norm}(x)), \qquad x'' = x' + \text{MLP}(\text{Norm}(x'))$$

This looks like a detail and is actually the whole game: the identity path x gives gradients a highway straight through every layer (Section 3.4 works the numbers). It also means the block is a delta writer — each layer adds a correction to the stream rather than overwriting it.

Because the shape never changes, the stream is shared memory: attention reads it, writes a delta; the MLP reads it, writes a delta. Interpretability researchers (Module 12) call this the "residual stream," and it is the single most useful mental model of how transformers actually compute.

Page 3 · Counting One Block

Where does the parameter budget actually go? For GPT-2 small (d = 768, 12 heads, MLP 768 → 3072):

$$\underbrace{3 \times 768 \times 768}_{\text{QKV}} + \underbrace{768 \times 768}_{\text{out proj}} + \underbrace{768 \times 3072}_{\text{MLP up}} + \underbrace{3072 \times 768}_{\text{MLP down}} \approx 7.1\text{M}$$

Per block: attention ≈ 2.36M params, MLP ≈ 4.72M. The feed-forward is 2× the attention — and in modern SwiGLU models the ratio gets worse (Section 3.6). When people say "the MLP is where the model stores knowledge," this is the budget they mean.

12 blocks × 7.1M ≈ 85M + embeddings (50,257 × 768 ≈ 38.6M) + position + norms ≈ 124M total — exactly the published GPT-2 small parameter count. You just derived a real model's size by hand.

🔮 Predict Before You Compute
In a GPT-2-small-sized block (d = 768, MLP 768 → 3072), which sublayer holds more trainable parameters?
The MLP wins, about 2:1. Attention: 3·768·768 (QKV) + 768·768 (projection) ≈ 2.36M. MLP: 768·3072 + 3072·768 ≈ 4.72M. The MLP is wider than the stream — it expands to 4× d and contracts back, and those two big matrices dominate the budget. This is why Llama-style models put their largest matrices in the feed-forward — and why MoE (Section 3.7) swaps exactly this sublayer for many smaller experts.

🔮 Predict Before You Compute
During "cat"'s journey through one block, which stage is the ONLY place its vector is influenced by the other tokens in the sequence?
Attention only. The MLP is position-wise: it runs the same function on each token's vector independently — token 0 never touches token 2 inside it. This asymmetry — attention exchanges information, MLP processes it — is the fundamental division of labor in every transformer.

The Parts

Three mechanisms keep the machine alive: nonlinearities that make it expressive, a highway that makes it trainable, and a ruler that keeps its signals in scale.

SECTION 3.3

The Activation Forge — ReLU, GELU, SiLU, SwiGLU

Without a nonlinearity, a stack of blocks collapses into one linear map — depth buys nothing. This forge casts the real curves of the activations that power GPT-2 (GELU), Llama (SiLU-gated SwiGLU), and every modern LLM, with live values and derivatives.

🎯 By the end of this section, you will be able to sketch ReLU, GELU, and SiLU by hand, state the difference between exact GELU and its tanh approximation, and explain what "gated" means in SwiGLU — why one branch filters, the other supplies content.
$$\text{ReLU}(x) = \max(0, x), \qquad \text{SiLU}(x) = x \cdot \sigma(x)$$
$$\text{GELU}(x) = x \cdot \Phi(x), \quad \Phi = \text{standard normal CDF}$$
$$\text{GELU}(x) \approx 0.5x\left(1 + \tanh\left(\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{\pi}}\,(x + 0.044715 x^3)\right)\right)$$

The lineage: ReLU (2011) killed the sigmoid's saturation problem with a hard kink. GELU (Hendrycks & Gimpel, 2016) smoothed the kink with a Gaussian gate — GPT-2 used it. SiLU (2017, a.k.a. Swish, β=1) multiplied x by its own sigmoid — smooth, non-monotonic, slightly negative for small x. Llama's SwiGLU (Shazeer, 2020) gates the MLP with SiLU — Section 3.6 assembles the full matrices.

$$\text{SwiGLU}(x) = \underbrace{\text{SiLU}(x W_g)}_{\text{gate}} \odot \underbrace{(x W_u)}_{\text{value}}$$

GELU and SiLU both gate x by a smooth value between 0 and 1: small inputs are softly suppressed, large positive inputs pass. This is the entire philosophy of modern activations — soft, differentiable selection — versus ReLU's hard zero. 📓 Instruction Manual · GELU: From Gaussian Gate to Tanh Shortcut

Page 1 · The Exact Definition

'16GELU multiplies the input by the probability that a standard normal random variable is ≤ x — a soft, probabilistic gate:

$$\text{GELU}(x) = x \cdot \Phi(x) = x \cdot \frac{1}{2}\left(1 + \text{erf}\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\right)$$

For x = 2: Φ(2) ≈ 0.9772, so GELU(2) ≈ 1.954 — the gate is nearly open. For x = 0: Φ(0) = 0.5, GELU(0) = 0. For x = −2: Φ(−2) ≈ 0.0228, GELU(−2) ≈ −0.0455 — a small negative passthrough, not a hard zero like ReLU. The Gaussian is what makes it smooth: no kink, everywhere-differentiable.

Page 2 · The Tanh Shortcut

In 2016, erf was comparatively slow on GPU kernels. Hendrycks & Gimpel published a closed-form approximation using tanh, with a cubic correction term:

$$\text{GELU}(x) \approx 0.5x\left(1 + \tanh\left(\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{\pi}}\,(x + 0.044715 x^3)\right)\right)$$

The 0.044715 cubic term is tuned to match Φ's sigmoid shape near 0 and its tails. The two functions agree to roughly 4 decimal places across the whole real line — the lab computes the worst-case gap live. GPT-2's PyTorch implementation ships exactly this approximation.

Rule of thumb: if you ever see 0.044715 in a codebase, that's GELU's tanh shortcut — one of the most-copied magic constants in deep learning.

Page 3 · Why Smoothness Matters

ReLU's derivative is a step: 1 for x > 0, 0 for x < 0 — gradient abruptly switches off for negative inputs ("dead ReLU"). GELU's derivative is smooth and never exactly zero: Φ(x) + x·φ(x) > 0 always. In a 100-block stack, millions of tiny gradient contributions from "almost-off" neurons still carry signal — and smoothness makes optimization trajectories more predictable.

The empirical lesson of 2016–2023: smooth activations train deeper networks more reliably. That's why the whole frontier moved from ReLU → GELU → SiLU, and why Llama's SwiGLU (SiLU inside) is now the default.

🔮 Predict Before You Compute
Slide the forge's inspection slider to x = −2. Which activation has a derivative that dips below zero there?
SiLU. Its derivative is σ(x) + x·σ(x)(1 − σ(x)). At x = −2: σ ≈ 0.119, so σ + x·σ(1−σ) ≈ 0.119 + (−2)(0.119)(0.881) ≈ 0.119 − 0.210 ≈ −0.091. A negative derivative means the function briefly decreases as x increases — SiLU dips to about −0.28 at x ≈ −1.28 before rising. ReLU's derivative is 0 on the left (never −1), and GELU's derivative is strictly positive. This little dip is harmless in practice — and a fantastic party fact about the activation inside every Llama.
SECTION 3.4

Residual Connections — The Gradient Highway

The "+" bricks from Section 3.1 look trivial: x + F(x) instead of F(x). They are the single most important training innovation since backprop. Here is the mechanism, computed layer by layer — and why transformers never concatenate instead.

🎯 By the end of this section, you will be able to derive the residual gradient recursion, explain why the identity path keeps gradient magnitude near 1 across 100 layers, and state why additive (not concatenative) residuals are the standard.
LAB 3.4 The highway — gradient magnitude per layer, with vs without
30
The theorem in one picture: without residuals the gradient at layer 1 is the product of 30 Jacobian factors and collapses to machine-zero. With residuals, each factor becomes (1 + ∂F) — the identity term keeps the product near 1 no matter how deep the stack. The lab uses seeded, schematic λ values for ∂F/∂x; the structure — product vs product-of-(1+·) — is the real mathematics.
$$x_{\ell+1} = x_{\ell} + F(x_{\ell}) \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{\partial L}{\partial x_{\ell}} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial x_{\ell+1}} \left(1 + \frac{\partial F}{\partial x_{\ell}}\right)$$

Chain the recursion from the top: the gradient reaching layer 1 is the loss gradient times the product of (1 + ∂F) across every layer above. Even if every ∂F is small, the 1 survives — the identity path is a highway that bypasses the entire stack. Without it, you multiply the raw ∂F factors, and any value below 1 raised to 30–100 layers vanishes to nothing.

🛣️
Why transformers can be 100+ layers deep while 2014-era networks stalled at ~20: the residual highway. The famous 2015 ResNet paper (He et al.) applied this to CNNs; transformers inherited it wholesale. Every failure you'll meet in Section 3.10's exam traces back to this one equation.
LAB 3.4b Additive vs concatenated — the dimension ledger
Additive keeps the stream at d dimensions forever — the next layer's matrix is d×d = 64 params in the toy. Concatenation doubles the stream every block: after 10 blocks, a d=8 stream is 8·2¹⁰ = 8,192 wide and the next matrix is 8,192×8. Additive residuals aren't a preference — they're the only way to stack.
🧮 Worked Example — The 3-Layer Recursion, By Hand

Setup: three layers with schematic Jacobian values ∂F₁ = 0.4, ∂F₂ = −0.2, ∂F₃ = 0.3. With residuals, each step contributes factor (1 + ∂F).

With: (1+0.4)(1−0.2)(1+0.3) = 1.4 × 0.8 × 1.3 = 1.456

Without residuals, the factors multiply raw:

Without: 0.4 × (−0.2) × 0.3 = −0.024

Extend to 30 layers of ±0.4-ish factors: the no-residual product scales like 0.6³⁰ ≈ 2×10⁻⁷ — the gradient reaching layer 1 is effectively zero, so the earliest layers never learn. The with-residual product wanders around O(1) because the identity terms dominate.

→ Ratio after 30 layers: roughly 10⁶–10⁷. That ratio IS the residual connection.

🔮 Predict Before You Compute
A 40-layer transformer is trained WITHOUT residual connections. Every layer's Jacobian factor has magnitude ≈ 0.6. The gradient reaching layer 1 is approximately…
Zero. The chain rule multiplies 40 factors of 0.6: 0.6⁴⁰ ≈ 1.3×10⁻⁹ — nine orders of magnitude below the gradient the output layer receives, far under the ~10⁻⁶ noise floor where an update is drowned by every other term in the optimizer. The first layers freeze; the model effectively trains only its top layers. This is the exact pathology the residual highway exists to prevent — and why every transformer since 2017 has it.
SECTION 3.5

Normalization — LayerNorm vs RMSNorm

Every sublayer sees a re-scaled input. Two rulers dominate: LayerNorm (subtract mean, divide by std) and RMSNorm (divide by RMS only). The difference is one subtraction — and it changes everything when the stream drifts.

LayerNorm subtracts the mean, so shifting the whole vector by c changes NOTHING. RMSNorm has no subtraction: a shifted vector has a bigger RMS, so the output shrinks. That is the entire difference — and the entire reason one is mean-invariant and the other isn't.
Do this: drag the mean-shift slider to +4 and watch. LayerNorm output: pixel-identical. RMSNorm output: visibly compressed. Then randomize the vector and try again. In production, embedding statistics drift over training — and this one subtraction is the difference between "invariant" and "drifting with the stream".
$$\text{LayerNorm}(x) = \frac{x - \mu}{\sqrt{\sigma^2 + \epsilon}} \odot \gamma + \beta, \qquad \mu = \tfrac{1}{d}\textstyle\sum x_i,\; \sigma^2 = \tfrac{1}{d}\textstyle\sum (x_i - \mu)^2$$

LayerNorm (Ba et al., 2016) centers and scales: output has mean 0, std 1 (before the learned affine γ, β). The subtraction of μ is what makes it invariant to adding a constant to every component.

$$\text{RMSNorm}(x) = \frac{x}{\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{d}\textstyle\sum x_i^2 + \epsilon}} \odot \gamma$$

RMSNorm (Zhang & Sennrich, 2019) drops the mean entirely: divide by RMS only. ~15% cheaper (no mean pass, no variance pass — one reduction instead of two), and for LLMs empirically just as good — as long as the stream's mean stays near zero. Llama, Mistral, Qwen, DeepSeek all use RMSNorm.

🧮 Worked Example — x = [2, −1, 3, 0], both rulers, then a mean shift

LayerNorm: μ = 1, σ² = (1+4+4+1)/4 = 2.5, σ = 1.581 → output = (x−1)/1.581 = [0.632, −1.265, 1.265, −0.632]. Mean 0, std 1.

LN(x) = (x − 1) / 1.581

RMSNorm: mean(x²) = (4+1+9+0)/4 = 3.5, RMS = 1.871 → output = x / 1.871 = [1.069, −0.535, 1.604, 0.000]. RMS of output = 1.

RMSNorm(x) = x / 1.871

Shift everything by +8: x' = [10, 7, 11, 8]. LayerNorm: μ = 9, same σ = 1.581 → output still [0.632, −1.265, 1.265, −0.632]. Identical. RMSNorm: mean(x'²) = 83.5, RMS = 9.137 → output = [1.094, 0.766, 1.204, 0.876]. Different — the shift leaked straight through.

→ Same vector, same shift: LN ignores it, RMSNorm doesn't. The lab's sliders show exactly this.

⚖️
Pre-norm vs post-norm: the 2017 original put LayerNorm after the residual add (post-norm). Modern models put it before the sublayer (pre-norm): Norm → Attention → +. Why? With pre-norm, the residual path carries the raw stream with no normalization applied — gradients flow through the identity untouched, and the norm only shapes the sublayer's input. Post-norm normalizes the stream itself, which fights the gradient highway. Pre-norm is one of the quiet reasons Llama trains at 100+ layers while the 2017 transformer needed careful warmup. Note: because the stream is never normalized, its magnitude can drift over training — which brings us right back to why mean-shifts matter for RMSNorm.
🔮 Predict Before You Compute
You add +8 to every component of an input vector, then apply LayerNorm and RMSNorm (γ = 1, β = 0). What happens to the two outputs?
LayerNorm identical, RMSNorm different. LN subtracts μ before dividing, so x+c and x produce the same centered vector. RMSNorm has no subtraction: RMS(x+c) is larger, so x+c divided by the bigger RMS comes out smaller — the shift leaks into the output. In a pre-norm Llama, the un-normalized stream drifts over training; models therefore keep the stream roughly zero-mean in practice (weight init and the embedding scale do this implicitly). This exact sensitivity is why Section 3.10's exam has a question about swapping one for the other.
Part III

The Feed-Forward

The MLP is where the model thinks — and where the parameter budget goes. Modern families make it gated (SwiGLU) or massive-but-sparse (MoE).

SECTION 3.6

The MLP Expansion — d → 4d → d

The feed-forward network projects each token up into a wide hidden space, activates it, and projects back down. Wide means expressive: 4× the stream width in the classic design. Modern Llama-style models gate that expansion with SwiGLU — three matrices where GPT-2 had two.

🎯 By the end of this section, you will be able to count the parameters of any MLP from its dimensions, state the SwiGLU decomposition (W_up, W_gate, W_down), and explain why the MLP costs 1.5× more parameters than a plain MLP of the same width — and why Llama pays it.
LAB 3.6 The expansion bench — d=8 in, 4d=32 hidden, d out, real weights
×1.00
Plain MLP: W_up (8×32 = 256) → GELU → W_down (32×8 = 256) = 512 params. SwiGLU: W_gate and W_up both 8×32, then element-wise gate ⊙ value, then W_down = 768 params — exactly 1.5×. That ratio is why MoE (Section 3.7) exists: when the MLP is the biggest brick, make many small MLPs and route.
$$\text{MLP}(x) = W_{down}\,\text{GELU}(W_{up}\,x) \qquad \text{(GPT-2)}$$
$$\text{SwiGLU}(x) = \left(\text{SiLU}(xW_{gate}) \odot (xW_{up})\right) W_{down} \qquad \text{(Llama)}$$

The gate branch SiLU(xW_g) learns which hidden neurons should speak for this token; the value branch xW_u supplies what they say; the product is the filtered message; W_down maps it back into the stream. Gating makes each token use a different sparse-ish subset of the hidden width — the same "softly select" philosophy as GELU, one level up.

🏭
What Llama actually runs: Llama-2 7B: d = 4,096, hidden = 11,008 (≈2.7×d). Llama-3 8B: d = 4,096, hidden = 14,336 (3.5×d). GPT-2 used the textbook 4× (768 → 3,072) with GELU. The "4×" rule is the classic design; modern models tune the ratio and always use SwiGLU — gating buys better quality per parameter than extra width alone (Shazeer, 2020).

🔮 Predict Before You Compute
SwiGLU adds a third matrix (W_gate) to the MLP. Compared to a plain MLP with the SAME hidden width, how many more parameters does the SwiGLU MLP have?
50% more. Plain: W_up (d×h) + W_down (h×d) = 2dh params. SwiGLU: W_gate (d×h) + W_up (d×h) + W_down (h×d) = 3dh — exactly 1.5×. Check the bench: 512 → 768. That's why MoE exists: when the MLP is already the biggest brick, the next step isn't a wider MLP — it's many smaller ones with a router.
SECTION 3.7

MoE — The Router's Job

Mixture of Experts replaces the one big MLP with many smaller experts and a router that sends each token to its best top-k. Same shape in, same shape out — but the forward pass only touches a fraction of the parameters. Here is the router math, computed on real numbers.

Softmax turns logits into a probability over experts. Top-k keeps only the k winners and renormalizes their weights so they sum to 1 (Mixtral-style). The load-balancing loss below measures how far the routing distribution is from uniform.
Watch the scale slider: higher logit scale = sharper softmax = more confident routing. The ledger simulates 300 tokens (logits + fixed seeded noise) and reports the fraction routed to each expert plus the auxiliary load-balancing loss — then hit Collapse to E3 to see what happens when the router forgets everyone else.
$$p_i = \text{softmax}(x W_{router})_i, \qquad y = \sum_{i \in \text{top-}k} \frac{p_i}{\sum_{j \in \text{top-}k} p_j} \, \text{Expert}_i(x)$$

The two-step trick: the router is a tiny linear layer that scores each expert; softmax converts scores to weights; top-k selects the winners; the winners' weights are renormalized so they sum to 1. The unselected experts contribute zero compute — that's the entire efficiency win.

🧮 Worked Example — Top-2 Renormalization

Logits for one token: [1.2, 0.6, 2.8, −0.4]. Softmax → [0.149, 0.082, 0.739, 0.030] (scale 1.0; the lab's 1.5 default sharpens this).

p = softmax([1.2, 0.6, 2.8, −0.4]) ≈ [0.149, 0.082, 0.739, 0.030]

Top-2: experts 3 (0.739) and 1 (0.149). Renormalize: 0.739/(0.739+0.149) ≈ 0.832, 0.149/0.888 ≈ 0.168.

w = [0.832, 0.168] → y = 0.832·E₃(x) + 0.168·E₁(x)

→ Experts 2 and 4 contributed nothing: 0 FLOPs from them. The model's capacity (46.7B params in Mixtral) is available, but only ~12.9B actually compute per token.

$$\mathcal{L}_{aux} = \alpha \cdot N \sum_{i=1}^{N} f_i \, p_i, \qquad f_i = \frac{\text{tokens routed to } i}{\text{total tokens}}$$

The load-balancing loss (Switch Transformer, Fedus et al. 2021): f_i is the observed fraction of tokens each expert actually receives, p_i the router's average probability for it. If routing is uniform, Σ f_i p_i = 1/N (balanced, small). If every token collapses to one expert, Σ f_i p_i = 1 — N× larger. With α = 0.01, the penalty is gentle when healthy and brutal when collapsed.

📊
Real routers: Mixtral 8x7B — 46.7B total, ~12.9B active, top-2 of 8 experts. DeepSeek-V3 — 671B total, 37B active, top-8 of 256 experts + 1 shared expert. GPT-4 is reported (unconfirmed — OpenAI never published) to route across ~16 experts with ~1.76T total and ~280B active. All three add an auxiliary load-balancing term to training, exactly as above.
📓 Instruction Manual · The MoE Router, Page by Page

Page 1 · Why Experts, Not a Wider MLP

'21A single MLP must serve every token with the same weights — every token pays the full 3dh parameter cost (Section 3.6). MoE's insight: different tokens want different specialists. A router picks a small committee per token, so the model can afford many experts without making every token pay for all of them. The parameter count explodes (Mixtral: 46.7B); the per-token compute barely moves (~12.9B active).

Memory grows with total parameters; compute grows with active parameters. MoE is a bet that memory is cheap and compute is not — true at datacenter scale, painful on a single GPU (your exam will revisit this).

Page 2 · The Load-Balancing Loss, Worked

Four experts, 300 tokens, α = 0.01. Healthy routing: f = [0.26, 0.24, 0.27, 0.23], p ≈ f → Σ fᵢpᵢ ≈ 0.25, L_aux ≈ 0.01·4·0.25 = 0.01. Collapsed routing: f = [0, 0, 1, 0], p = [0, 0, ~1, 0] → Σ = 1, L_aux ≈ 0.01·4·1 = 0.04 — 4× larger, and gradient descent will push the router back toward fairness.

$$f_i = \frac{\text{tokens routed to expert } i}{\text{total tokens}}, \qquad \mathcal{L}_{aux} = \alpha N \textstyle\sum_i f_i p_i$$

The trick that makes it trainable: f depends on the argmax (not differentiable), but p depends on the softmax (differentiable) — so the loss gently moves probabilities, which moves the argmax distribution. The lab computes all of this live.

Page 3 · Capacity, Dropout, and the Shared Expert

Production routers add a capacity factor: each expert processes at most (tokens/experts)×C per batch, C ≈ 1.25. Overflowing tokens are dropped (or sent to a fallback) — this caps worst-case latency. DeepSeek-V3 adds one shared expert that every token always uses (common knowledge lives there), while 256 routed experts handle specialization. The auxiliary loss formula above, with α tuned per model, is the standard guard against the collapse you can trigger in the lab.

When you see "8×7B" or "671B" next to a small "active" number, you're looking at an MoE — and now you know the exact mechanism: softmax → top-k → renormalize → per-token committee.

🔮 Predict Before You Compute
A router without a load-balancing loss collapses: 97% of tokens route to expert 3. What happens to the model?
The other experts starve. Tokens never routed to an expert contribute zero gradient through it — the expert's weights never update and it stays at initialization forever. You end up with one MLP doing the work of a model that paid for four. The load-balancing loss exists precisely to prevent this: in the lab, hit Collapse to E3 and watch L_aux jump 4× — that's the training signal that would push the router back to fairness. Collapsed routers are a real, documented failure mode in MoE training (and in your Section 3.10 exam).

📓 Instruction Manual · The Shapes of the Forward Pass

Page 1 · The Four Tensors

'23For a sequence of T tokens with hidden size d, every stage is a tensor you can name. Embedding: T×d. Queries, keys, values per head: T×(d_k). Scores: T×T — one number per (query, key) pair. Attention output: T×d. The entire forward pass is these shapes flowing through the machine, and every shape change is either a matmul (T×d times d×d) or a per-token operation (norms, MLP, activation).

When engineers say "the transformer is shape-disciplined," this is what they mean: you can always predict the next tensor's shape from the last one. The simulator shows each shape at every step.

Page 2 · Masked Attention

Language models predict the next token, so token i may only attend to tokens j ≤ i. The causal mask writes −∞ into the scores matrix above the diagonal — after softmax those entries become exactly 0. The simulator computes the masked scores matrix and shows the final attention weights per token, row by row.

$$\text{weights}_{i,j} = \frac{\exp(s_{i,j} / \sqrt{d_k})}{\sum_{k \leq i} \exp(s_{i,k} / \sqrt{d_k})} \quad \text{for } j \leq i, \text{ else } 0$$

Notice the sum runs only over j ≤ i — each row is its own little softmax over the past. This is the exact causal machinery of every decoder-only LLM (GPT-2 through DeepSeek).

Page 3 · From Logits to a Token

The last hidden state of the last token is multiplied by the output head (V×d in our toy, 128,256×4,096 in Llama-3) to produce logits — one raw score per vocabulary entry. Softmax converts them to probabilities; sampling or argmax picks the next token; that token is appended to the sequence and the whole loop repeats. One forward pass = one step of generation.

The loop is the model: forward pass → sample → append → forward pass again. Everything you'll build in Module 05's training arena is this loop plus a loss. Congratulations — you now hold the entire architecture in your head at once.

🔮 Predict Before You Compute
After the simulator computes logits and applies softmax over the 14-token vocabulary for the final token, the probabilities sum to…
Exactly 1 (up to float rounding). Softmax divides by the sum of its own exponentials — the output is a probability distribution by definition, so Σ p = 1 no matter what the logits were. This is the same guarantee behind every LLM's sampling step and behind why "next-token probability" is a meaningful number. (The tiny float32 residue, ~10⁻⁷, is why we say "exactly 1 up to rounding" — check the simulator's readout at the Logits stage.) If you predicted "near 1", you were right in spirit — and you've just internalized the one invariant that makes the whole machine speak in probabilities.
