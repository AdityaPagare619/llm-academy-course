Part I

The Failure

Before we build the library, we have to see why a brain full of knowledge is not enough - and why the failure is so easy to miss.

SECTION 8.1

Why Models Hallucinate — Parametric vs Non-Parametric Memory

Everything an LLM "knows" is compressed into its weights during training. That memory is called parametric - and it has a shelf life, a privacy problem, and a long tail. Retrieval is the non-parametric answer: a library outside the weights.

🎯 By the end of this section, you will be able to explain what parametric memory is, why it fails exactly where facts get fresh or private, and name the three failure modes retrieval exists to fix.

1 · The Cutoff

Training data ends at a date. The model knows nothing after it. Ask about last week's events and it will either refuse or - worse - confidently invent them. Retrieval injects fresh facts at query time.

2 · The Private Gap

Your documents, your codebase, your company wiki - the model has never seen them. Fine-tuning them into the weights is slow and leaks. Retrieval keeps private data out of the weights and in the context instead.

3 · The Long Tail

Obscure facts appear too rarely in training to be learned reliably. The model memorizes the head of the distribution and hallucinates the tail. Retrieval finds the needle instead of hoping it was memorized.

Hallucination taxonomy (Weng, Jul 2024): in-context hallucination = the model contradicts the context you gave it (fixable with better grounding). The honest goal is not zero hallucination - it is a model that knows what it doesn't know and retrieves, or says "I'm not sure".

Why "parametric"? The knowledge lives in the parameters - the billions of weights. Reading it back is probabilistic generation: the model doesn't query a database, it produces the most plausible continuation. When the fact isn't in the weights, the most plausible continuation is still produced - it is just wrong. That's the confident-wrong problem: fluency and accuracy are decoupled.

🗄️
The fix in one sentence: don't make the model know everything - make it look things up. A library outside the weights (a corpus, an index) can be updated in seconds, kept private, and cited. Retrieval-Augmented Generation is exactly that: retrieval supplies the facts, generation supplies the fluency.

The honest framing

GPT-4, Claude, and Llama hallucinate on fresh facts because nobody's weights update on news time. The 2023-2026 generation added browsing, tools, and retrieval for exactly this reason. Module 08 builds the retrieval part from scratch; Module 09 builds the tool-using agent on top of it.

🔮 Predict Before You Compute
You ask a model about an event that happened one week after its training cutoff. What does it most likely do?
It answers - fluently, specifically, and wrong. The model was trained to produce the most plausible next token, not to check whether its knowledge exists. Outside the training distribution it doesn't detect "missing" - it generates. This is the exact failure the rest of the module fixes: give it a library, and make it check. (Abstention is a real research direction - "I don't know" is trained in - but it is the exception, not the default.)
SECTION 8.2

The Library Idea — RAG at a Glance

Retrieval-Augmented Generation: retrieve the relevant text, augment the prompt with it, generate the answer grounded in it. Three stages, three bricks - and a pipeline you will assemble brick by brick in 8.6.

$$\text{RAG}(q) = \text{Generate}\Big( \text{Prompt}\big(q,\; \text{Retrieve}(q, \mathcal{D})\big) \Big)$$

The A in RAG is Augment: the retrieved text is injected into the prompt before the model writes a single token. No new weights, no retraining, no fine-tuning - the model stays frozen, and the knowledge changes underneath it. That decoupling is the whole point: what the model knows becomes a data problem, not a training problem.

🧲
Why "grounding"? A generated answer is grounded when every claim it makes is supported by text the model can point to. That's why RAG systems can show citations: you know exactly which chunks the model saw. The module's title - the Grounding Chamber - is the place where answers are bolted to evidence before they leave the factory.
📏
The RAG quality triad (O'Reilly 2024): (1) Relevance - are the right chunks retrieved at all? (2) Information density - do they contain the answer, or just the topic? (3) Detail level - enough specifics to answer precisely? Most RAG failures are retrieval failures, not generation failures. Fix retrieval first - sections 8.3-8.5 build the pieces, 8.8 measures them.
📖 Instruction Manual — Retrieval Meets Generation: a Short History (2017-2023)

2017 — The Brain Is Born Without a Library

2017"Attention Is All You Need" introduces the transformer. The model's only knowledge is what training compressed into its weights. Nobody calls it "parametric memory" yet - but that's what it is: knowledge that cannot be updated without retraining. The idea that the model should look things up exists only on the search-engine side of the field.

The original transformer has no retrieval mechanism at all. Every fact it will ever "know" is decided on the day training ends.

2020 — The Paper That Names It

"Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks" (Lewis et al., Facebook AI) fuses a dense retriever with a seq2seq generator and trains them together. The same year, REALM (Guu et al.) and DPR (Karpukhin et al.) make retrieval a first-class citizen: pre-train with a retrieval step, and the model learns to use external text. The acronym RAG enters the lexicon.

Key idea from 2020: the retriever and the generator can be trained as one system - the model learns which text helps it answer. Modern RAG pipelines still separate them for engineering reasons, but the joint view is the origin.

2021 — Retrieval Becomes a Product Feature

WebGPT (OpenAI) shows a model browsing the web to answer questions, with citations in its training signal. For the first time, "look it up before you answer" is not an academic trick but a product behavior - and the citations are part of the output contract, because users need to check the model's work.

2022 — The Confident-Wrong Era Goes Mainstream

ChatGPT ships with purely parametric memory. It is fluent, brilliant, and hallucinates with total confidence - and suddenly millions of people notice. The failures that Part I describes become dinner-table conversation. The fix was already published; now it becomes urgent.

2023 — The Grounding Year

Bing Chat and Perplexity ship search-grounded chat: every answer carries sources. GPT-4 gains browsing. "Grounding" becomes the product word for what retrieval does - and the community starts counting tokens, chunk sizes, and recall rates. That's the world Module 08 prepares you for: the pipeline is no longer exotic; it is the default way to ship a fact-facing LLM product.

Your turn: after this module you can rebuild this entire timeline's ending - a grounded answer with citations - with six bricks and a toy corpus, and know exactly which part of the pipeline each real system is optimizing when it reports better numbers.

Part II

The Pieces

A library is only as good as its indexing. Three problems decide whether a lookup succeeds: how the books are split, how they are filed, and how fast you can find them.

SECTION 8.3

Chunking — The Splitting Problem

Retrieval works on pieces, not whole documents. Too big and each piece is a blur of noise; too small and the piece loses its context. This section makes the tradeoff visible with real word-level splitting you control.

🎯 By the end of this section, you will be able to split a document into chunks by hand, compute chunk counts with overlap, and predict how chunk size bends retrieval quality.
🧮 Worked Example — Chunking by Hand: 20 Words, Size 6, Overlap 2

Our passage has 20 words. With chunk size s = 6 and overlap o = 2, each chunk starts step = s - o = 4 words after the previous one. The starts are words 1, 5, 9, 13, 17:

C1: words 1-6 · C2: words 5-10 · C3: words 9-14 · C4: words 13-18 · C5: words 17-20 (partial)

Chunk C1 is "The remedy is not a bigger"; C2 is "a bigger model but a library" - words 5-6 ("a bigger") appear in both. That overlap is deliberate: a fact straddling the boundary survives in at least one chunk.

The general count formula: with N words, size s, overlap o, and step = s - o:

#chunks = ⌈(N − s) / (s − o)⌉ + 1  →  ⌈(20 − 6) / 4⌉ + 1 = ⌈3.5⌉ + 1 = 5

→ 5 chunks. Duplicated words: overlap × (#chunks − 1) = 2 × 4 = 8 words stored twice (40% of the document re-indexed). Overlap is insurance, and insurance has a price.

$$\text{chunk}_i = \text{words}[\, i \cdot (s-o) \;:\; i \cdot (s-o) + s \,], \qquad \#\text{chunks} = \left\lceil \frac{N - s}{s - o} \right\rceil + 1$$

Two failure modes, one knob. Too large: the chunk matches a query on topic, but the actual answer sits in a sea of unrelated sentences - the retrieval score says "relevant", the generation reads noise, and the answer dilutes or disappears. Too small: the chunk loses pronouns, qualifiers, and context - and a fact split across the boundary is invisible to both chunks that hold its halves.

⚖️
The production rule of thumb: most teams start around 200-500 tokens per chunk with 10-20% overlap, then tune on their own data - chunk size is a hyperparameter, not a law. Tokenizers (Module 01) make token counts the real currency: roughly 1 token per English word (approx), so 200-500 tokens ≈ 150-350 words.
🔪
Why not whole documents? A 5,000-word document about a company contains thousands of facts; a question about one of them must compete against all the others for the top-k slots. Whole-document retrieval is like a librarian who hands you the entire shelf because one book might help. Chunking makes the library's units match the scale of questions.

The boundary tax

Every boundary is a place where a fact can be cut in half. Overlap is the cheapest insurance: a sentence that straddles a boundary appears in both neighboring chunks, so retrieval can find it from either side. Real systems also split on paragraph or sentence boundaries when they can - structure beats fixed word counts, when structure exists.

📖 Instruction Manual — Chunk Arithmetic

Page 1 · The Split Formula

Take a 100-word document, size s = 10, overlap o = 2. Each chunk starts step = 8 words after the last:

$$\#\text{chunks} = \left\lceil \frac{100 - 10}{10 - 2} \right\rceil + 1 = \lceil 11.25 \rceil + 1 = 12 + 1 = 13$$

Starts at words 1, 9, 17, ..., 97 - thirteen chunks covering the document with 2-word seams. As a sanity check: 13 chunks × 10 words = 130 word-slots for a 100-word document; the extra 30 are the overlap (12 duplicated seams of 2 words, plus partial final chunk edge effects).

Token estimate: ~1 token per English word (approx), so a 10-word chunk ≈ 10 tokens. When a prompt has a 2,000-token budget, that's ≈ 200 such chunks (Section 8.7 puts a budget on this exact arithmetic).

Page 2 · Why Overlap Exists

With o = 0, a fact that straddles a boundary is split in half: "the treaty was signed in / Versailles on 28 June" - neither chunk contains the fact, and keyword matching fails on both. With overlap o = 2, the boundary sentence appears in both chunks, so a query for "treaty" or "Versailles" finds the complete sentence.

$$\text{duplicate words} = o \times (\#\text{chunks} - 1) \quad \Rightarrow \quad 2 \times 12 = 24 \text{ words re-indexed (24\%)}$$

Overlap costs index space and slightly inflated similarity scores for boundary words. It buys boundary safety. The right overlap depends on how often your facts cross arbitrary word boundaries - which is "often", which is why 10-20% is the common starting point.

Page 3 · The Tradeoff Table

There is no perfect size - only the right failure mode for your data:

Too big (500+ words): retrieval matches on topic, the answer drowns in noise, generation copies irrelevant detail, prompt budget burns fast. Too small (under ~50 words): context lost, pronouns dangle, facts get split at boundaries, and you need more chunks (and a bigger top-k) to cover one answer.

The honest production answer: 200-500 tokens with 10-20% overlap is where teams start (rule of thumb, not a law); then they measure retrieval quality (Section 8.8) on their own corpus and tune. Chunk size is a lever you pull while watching precision@k - not a constant you memorize.

$$\text{sim}(q, d) = \underbrace{\frac{\langle q, d \rangle}{\|q\| \, \|d\|}}_{\text{cosine similarity}} = \cos(\theta_{q,d})$$

Hover the terms above: the dot product ⟨q, d⟩ measures how much the query and document point the same way; the norms ‖q‖ ‖d‖ divide out each vector's length so only direction counts. Cosine similarity runs from −1 (opposite) through 0 (unrelated) to +1 (identical direction). Every ranking in this section uses exactly this formula.

The only new idea is the bi-encoder: one encoder maps queries, another maps documents, both into the same space, so a question can land near its answer without sharing a single word.
🔎
Why meaning beats keywords: search "how do I fix a flat tire?" - keyword search finds pages containing those exact words. Semantic search finds "repairing a punctured bicycle wheel", because the embeddings are close in meaning-space. That is not a bug: it is the entire reason modern search and RAG systems embed at all.

At scale: ANN, not brute force

Comparing a query to billions of documents one by one is too slow. Real systems use Approximate Nearest Neighbor indexes (FAISS, HNSW, ScaNN) that trade a little accuracy for ~1000× speed (approx). This 2-D demo is brute force - exact but tiny; Section 8.5 makes the trade visible.

📖 Instruction Manual — The ANN Tradeoff

Page 1 · The Brute-Force Bill

Exact nearest-neighbor search scores every vector: N × d multiply-adds per query. With 50 vectors it is nothing (0.4 ms in your browser). With a production corpus:

$$1{,}000{,}000 \text{ docs} \times 384 \text{ dims} = 384 \times 10^6 \text{ ops per query}$$

At 10 queries per second that is ~3.8 billion ops/second just for scoring - before ranking, filtering, or the rest of the pipeline. And it is not only compute: every scored vector must be read from memory, so the real bottleneck is memory bandwidth (Module 11's serving dock measures exactly this kind of bill).

Page 2 · The Coarse Index

The trick is to never score what probably won't win. Quantize the space into cells (or clusters): each vector is filed into its nearest cell, and a query probes only the query's cell plus a few neighbors. Work per query drops from N to (N / cells) × probes.

The miss is honest: a true neighbor sitting in an unprobed cell is simply never seen. More probes → fewer misses → more work. Your lab's recall curve is the measured shape of that trade. HNSW's upgrade: multi-layer proximity graphs, where each layer is a coarser "cell" and the walk narrows in on the answer - same deal, far better constant.

Page 3 · Why 99% Is Enough

Recall@10 of ~95-99% at 100-1000× speedup is the common production operating point (vendor benchmarks, approx). It works because the pipeline does not need the exact top-10 - it needs the answer present somewhere in the top-k that the generator reads. A missed #4 that would not have changed the answer costs nothing; the 1000× speedup buys the whole product.

The disciplined habit: measure recall on your own queries before trusting an index (8.8 builds the ruler), and remember the failure mode you accept - not "slightly worse ranking" but "the right chunk sometimes absent" - which is exactly the kind of miss Section 8.10's exam asks you to diagnose.

$$\text{context} = \text{system} + \sum_{i=1}^{k} \text{chunk}_i + \text{question}, \qquad \text{used} = \sum \text{len}(\text{chunk}_i) \le \text{budget}$$

The budget discipline: retrieval and context are two knobs that must be turned together. Too much retrieval and the window overflows - chunks get cut (usually from the tail, which may hold the answer). Too little and the answer never arrives. The classic production move: retrieve to fit - pick k so that top-k chunks fit the budget with room for the template.

🚨
Truncation = silent hallucination: when a chunk is cut, the model doesn't know it was cut. It simply generates with the context it has - and where the answer is missing, its parametric memory fills the gap. Context overflow is not a UI annoyance; it is a grounding failure that looks exactly like the failures from Part I. The exam's fifth incident is this one.
📐
Why RAG still wins the budget war: a 1M-token window can hold whole books - but at quadratic attention cost (Module 04's wall), and with "lost in the middle" degrading real accuracy. Retrieval's bet is the opposite: put the three relevant pages in a small window instead of 500 irrelevant ones. Long context complements RAG; it doesn't replace it.

🔮 Predict Before You Compute
The context window holds 1,000 tokens. Retrieval returns 8 chunks totaling 1,240 tokens. What does the pipeline do?
It truncates - and that is where answers die. Context windows are hard limits: the pipeline keeps chunks in rank order until the budget is full and silently drops the rest. If the answer lives in a dropped chunk, the model never sees it and falls back on parametric memory - hallucinating with the exact confidence of Part I. The fix is upstream: budget the retrieval to the window before you retrieve (choose k by token count, not by hope).
Part IV

The Judgments

Retrieval is a hypothesis; quality is the test. Measure it with real numbers - then make it stronger by fusing two imperfect retrievers into one better one.

SECTION 8.8

Retrieval Quality — Precision and Recall, Measured Live

"Good retrieval" is not a vibe. Given a query, a set of relevant documents, and a ranked list, precision@k and recall@k turn quality into numbers you can tune against. This section's worked example computes them by hand - then the lab computes them live as you mark documents relevant.

🎯 By the end of this section, you will be able to compute precision@k and recall@k by hand and judge a retriever with real numbers instead of vibes.
🧮 Worked Example — Grading a Ranked List by Hand

A query has exactly 3 relevant documents in the corpus: {d2, d5, d7}. Our retriever returns this ranking: d1, d2, d5, d7, d8, d3, d4, d6. We inspect the top-k window - say k = 5: the window is {d1, d2, d5, d7, d8}.

top-5 = [d1, d2, d5, d7, d8]  ·  relevant ∩ top-5 = {d2, d5, d7} → 2 of the 3 are in the window

Precision asks: of what we returned, how much was relevant? Recall asks: of what was relevant, how much did we return?

P@5 = |relevant ∩ top-5| / 5 = 2/5 = 0.40  ·  R@5 = |relevant ∩ top-5| / |relevant| = 2/3 ≈ 0.667

→ Two different truths: the window is 40% relevant (half of it is noise), and we recovered 67% of the right answers. Precision and recall trade against each other as k grows - the lab below makes that trade visible on every slider move.

k = 5 · relevant 3 · P@5 = 0.40 · R@5 = 0.667. Most RAG failures are low recall (the right chunk never retrieved); low precision is cheaper - it just wastes budget.
$$\mathrm{P@}k = \frac{|\mathrm{relevant} \cap \mathrm{top\text{-}}k|}{k} \qquad \mathrm{R@}k = \frac{|\mathrm{relevant} \cap \mathrm{top\text{-}}k|}{|\mathrm{relevant}|}$$

Which one should you watch? In RAG, low recall is the deadly failure: if the right chunk never enters the top-k, no amount of generation quality can save the answer - it literally is not in the context. Low precision is wasteful (noise costs tokens and distracts the model) but survivable. That asymmetry is why retrieval-first debugging - the O'Reilly triad from 8.2 - starts with "did we retrieve the right chunk at all?"

🧪
How production measures it: teams build a labeled evaluation set - queries with known-good chunk ids - and report mean P@k / R@k (or MRR, nDCG for ranked quality) across it. Chunk size, overlap, index type, and fusion strategy (8.9) are all tuned against these numbers. The lab you just ran is that evaluation loop, shrunk to 8 documents.
⚖️
One honest caveat: "relevant" is a judgment call - two annotators may disagree on which documents answer a query. Production evaluation sets average over several annotators to smooth that noise. The formula is exact; the labels carry human uncertainty. Never let the clean math hide the messy labeling.
🔮 Predict Before You Compute
Exactly 2 of the 5 top-ranked documents are relevant to the query. What is precision@5?
0.40. Precision@k = relevant-in-window / window-size: 2/5 = 0.40. The denominator is always k - irrelevant returns still cost retrieval budget and generation attention, so they must dilute the score. (Recall would ask the other question: 2 over the total relevant documents - a different number, measured by a different ruler.)
SECTION 8.9

Hybrid Retrieval & Reranking — Two Imperfect Retrievers, One Better Ranking

Lexical search (BM25-style keywords) and dense search (embeddings) fail on different queries. Fusing their rankings with Reciprocal Rank Fusion keeps both wins - a real formula, computed live on six documents.

🎯 By the end of this section, you will be able to fuse two retrievers with RRF, explain why hybrid beats single, and name the systems that run this in production.

Read the bench: d5 ranks #3 lexically but #1 densely - fusion promotes it to the winner. d1 and d2 tie in RRF score (0.0318 - the rank-pair symmetry artifact) and the tiebreak goes to the better dense rank. Fused order: d5 → d2 → d1 → d3 → d4 → d6.
RRF (k = 60): d5 = 1/63 + 1/61 = 0.03227 wins. d1 = d2 = 0.03175 (tie, broken by dense rank). Hybrid keeps lexical wins and semantic wins.
$$\mathrm{RRF}(d) = \frac{1}{k + \mathrm{rank}_{lex}(d)} + \frac{1}{k + \mathrm{rank}_{dense}(d)}$$
term 1 · lexical rank term 2 · dense rank k = 60 smoothing

Hover a chip to highlight its retriever's bars on the canvas. RRF ignores scores entirely - it fuses ranks, which keeps two retrievers with different score scales comparable. k is a smoothing constant (60 is the standard starting point): it caps how much a single rank-1 can dominate.

🔀
Why hybrid beats single: lexical search nails exact strings - product codes, names, quotes, technical jargon - but misses paraphrases ("fix a flat" vs "repair a punctured tire"). Dense search nails meaning but can miss rare exact tokens. Their failures are disjoint, so a rank fusion inherits both strengths. In production this is the standard: BM25 + dense + RRF (or a learned reranker on top).
🧮 Worked Example — RRF by Hand, k = 60

Ranks: d5 is #3 lexically and #1 densely; d3 is #1 lexically and #6 densely. Both contribute one term per retriever:

RRF(d5) = 1/(60+3) + 1/(60+1) = 0.01587 + 0.01639 = 0.03227

RRF(d3) = 1/(60+1) + 1/(60+6) = 0.01639 + 0.01515 = 0.03154 - d5 beats d3 despite d3's lexical rank-1, because d5 is excellent in both lists.

d1 (ranks 2, 4) and d2 (ranks 4, 2) produce symmetric pairs: both score 1/62 + 1/64 = 0.03175. Exact tie - a genuine RRF artifact:

RRF(d1) = 1/62 + 1/64 = 0.03175 = RRF(d2) = 1/64 + 1/62

→ Rank-only fusion cannot separate symmetric pairs. Production systems break ties with a secondary signal (here: the better dense rank wins). The tie is not a bug - it is a fingerprint of the method, and spotting it is exactly the kind of judgment 8.10 will test.

🌐
Who runs this in production (labeled approx): Bing Chat (2023) fused web search - a lexical-style index - with LLM generation, and cited its sources; Perplexity's answer engine runs multi-source retrieval with reranking; enterprise RAG stacks (LangChain, LlamaIndex defaults) combine BM25 + dense embeddings with RRF exactly as this lab computes it, at web scale. Two-stage systems go further: retrieve 100 candidates cheaply, then re-score the top ~20 with a slow, accurate cross-encoder that reads query and document together. Fast recall, then precise ranking - the same division of labor you just watched on six documents.
🔮 Predict Before You Compute
When does hybrid retrieval (lexical + dense fused) beat either single retriever?
When their failures are disjoint. Fusion inherits strengths only if the retrievers are weak on different queries. A keyword retriever and an embedding retriever are near-perfect complements by construction: one is blind to paraphrase, the other to exact rare tokens. If both retrievers were the same kind, fusion would just double the same blind spot - which is why RRF is measured (8.8), not assumed, in production.
